# User Guide and Support

Scivelle is a word processor for novelists on the Mac: Word documents on real pages, your cast and your
notes beside the text, and a writing assistant on the page — Apple's own on-device model, or
the AI service you already use.

**Support.** This page is the User Guide, with every feature explained. Questions, problems and
ideas: write to [support@scivelle.com](mailto:support@scivelle.com). The [privacy policy](privacy)
says what the app does with your writing (in short: your writing leaves your Mac only for a service you choose,
when you use it). These pages also live at
[scivelle.com/guide](https://www.scivelle.com/guide) and [scivelle.com/privacy](https://www.scivelle.com/privacy).

---

Scivelle is a word processor for novelists on the Mac. It opens and saves Word documents,
lays your book out on real pages as you write, keeps the cast and the world of your story
beside the text, and brings a writing assistant to the page — Apple's own on-device model, or
the writing service you already use.

This guide covers everything in the app. The first three sections are enough to start writing.

---

## 1. Getting started

**A thesis or essay in a citation style.** The wizard's citation style sets the page: APA 7 gives
Letter paper, 1-inch margins, double spacing, the page number at the top right of every page, a
student title page (title in bold, then author, department, degree, supervisor, year), centred bold
Level 1 headings and a *References* page; MLA 9 gives the surname and page number together at the
top right, the first-page block instead of a title page and *Works Cited*; Chicago gives the title a
third of the way down the title page, centred headings and *Bibliography*. Harvard has no manual of
its own and IEEE governs only the references, so those take sensible defaults and your faculty's
word. The pane's Format card lists every rule of the style — ticked when Scivelle keeps it, open
when it is yours to keep by hand (first-line indents, block quotes, hanging indents) — with a
switch of style and *Apply … format* (named for the style: *Apply APA 7th format*), which puts the page back to the style's rules without touching
a word. In a thesis every chapter and every front- and back-matter section starts a new page.

**The ribbon knows what you are writing.** What you chose on the start screen shapes the tools:
a novel or a short story has them all; a thesis does without the story tools (Brainstorm,
Continue Writing, Create a Character) and the Characters tab; an essay also does without chapter
headers and the Outline, so its pane is Notes, Ask and Universe; a plain document keeps its Outline. The
pane's button on the Home tab is named for the kind (Manuscript, Thesis, Essay, Outline). Each kind
keeps to its own tools: the cast, scenes, the plot grid and the story tools are a novel's or a short
story's, and View ▸ All Ribbon Tools shows a kind's own hidden controls but never brings them into a
thesis, an essay or a document. The pane's heading says what the document is and changes it — Novel,
Short story, Thesis, Essay or Document — so a manuscript opened as a file becomes a novel there (a
student licence is offered the novelist's upgrade). A document, a paper or an essay has sections, not
chapters: no "Chapter 3" in the outline, no "End of Chapter" on the page, its headings on the page.
A file opened as it is (not begun here) is read for what it is — chapters and dialogue, a list
of sources and citations, a title page that calls it a thesis, or, when the text leaves room for
doubt, the assistant's one-word answer — and the pane offers it once: *This reads like a novel.
Make it a novel?* Nothing changes until you take it.

**The format a work is sent in.** A novel or a short story is held to standard manuscript format,
what agents and publishers ask for: US Letter, one-inch margins, 12-point Times New Roman (Courier
is accepted), double-spaced with no space between paragraphs, each chapter on a new page with its
title a third of the way down, and "Surname / TITLE" with the page number at the top right; the
novel wizard sets it up. A thesis, an essay or a paper is held to its citation style. The pane's
Format card (*Submission format* for fiction) lists the rules, says what in the document does not
match them now — the paper, the margins, the face and size, the spacing, the running head, chapters
running on, text set by hand in another font and in which chapter, a missing References or Works
Cited, a thesis without an Abstract — and *Apply manuscript format* (or *Apply … format* for the style) puts right all it can. Once
the document keeps everything Apply sets, the button reads *✓ Manuscript format applied* and rests
until something stops matching.

A novel or a story can be held to another submission format from the card's list instead:
- **Classic manuscript (Courier):** the standard format typed in 12-point Courier.
- **UK and Commonwealth agents (A4):** A4 paper, 12-point Times New Roman or Arial, 1.5 line
  spacing (double is accepted too).
- **Competition, judged anonymously:** your name nowhere in the manuscript. The running head is
  the TITLE and the page number, and the title page has the title and the word count only.
- **Custom format:** your own choices, for a publisher's or a prize's house rules. Set the paper,
  font, size, spacing, indent, page number and running head, and whether each chapter starts a
  page and there is a title page. It starts from the format you had, is kept with the document,
  and *Apply your format* sets the page by it.

Once everything matches, *Close the format card* folds it to one line ("✓ Matches …") so the
outline has the panel; the – at the card's corner folds it at any time. The folded line still says
when something stops matching, and a click opens the card again. Before a review panel
reads the work, the same check runs: what does not match is listed section by section, with *Fix
the format*, *Start the panel anyway*, or *Not now*; once everything matches, the panel convenes.

**The document's details.** Click the document's name at the top of the window for its title,
the author's name (or pen name) and the contact details an agent reads on a manuscript's title
page. They are kept in the document; the running head, the title page's lines and the shelf
follow them, and a document not yet saved takes the title as its name. *Apply manuscript format*
then makes the title page — the word count at the top right, the contact details at the top
left, the title and "by" the author halfway down — and sets the paragraphs' half-inch first-line
indent. On the Format card each rule is ticked as the document stands now: ✓ kept, ✗ not kept
(a click on the details rule opens them), ○ yours to keep by hand.

**Open the app.** The start screen asks what you would like to do: read a book, write a novel,
a thesis or an essay, or something else (a short story, a blank page, a file you have). Below
it, the shelves: *Writing*, the manuscripts begun here; your recent files; and at the bottom
*Reading*, the books you brought in, each with its cover and how far you got. Put them in your own
order: point at a shelf and drag the grip at the left of its title (or focus the grip and press ↑
or ↓); Scivelle keeps the order. Click *Writing* or *Reading* to open the Library at that shelf,
with all of it there to search and sort. The title bar reads *Start Page*
there (*Scivelle Wizard* while a set-up wizard runs), with no document name and none of the
document's controls, since nothing has been created yet. File ▸ Start Screen (⇧⌘N) brings it
back at any time; Home ▸ New gives a blank page straight away. *Settings*, at the top right of the start
screen, opens at the writing assistant, so a writing service can be connected before anything is written. The first button on the title bar (⇧⌘N) brings the start screen back from any document; if the document has unsaved changes you are asked to save first, go without saving, or stay.

**Setting up a manuscript.** The Start Page's *Novel Writings* (a novel or a short story) and *Academia Writings* (a thesis or an essay) open a short
wizard, a step at a time, with what writers and publishers settle before the first line. Its first
step, *Kind*, shows each kind as a book's cover or a paper's first page with a word on what it sets
up: click one to go on (Back returns to it to choose again). A
novel: title, author, genre and target length; the premise, point of view and tense; the cast,
each with a role, what they want and their flaw (they go to the Characters tab); the places, each
with its kind, where it is, what it is like and why the story needs it (they go to the Places tab),
and the time and the rules of the world (the Setting note in the Notes tab); the plot on a structure — three acts, Save the Cat's
fifteen beats, or the hero's journey — whose beats become sticky notes on the chapters in the
outline; and the chapters and the format, standard manuscript (12-point Times, double-spaced,
one-inch margins, author and title in the running head) or a printed book's look. A thesis:
the title page (degree, institution, supervisor, year), the research question, the sections
(an empirical IMRaD sequence, a humanities one, or your own) with their usual subsections, the
front and back matter, the citation style and the faculty's format. An essay: the question,
your name (the Mac account's to start with), your teacher or professor and the course — on the
first page as the citation style places them — the thesis statement, the points that carry it,
a word limit, the citation style. The Format card says so if a paper's first page does not
carry your name. Everything
can be changed afterwards. With AutoSave on (the default), the new document goes into Scivelle's
Library on its first change; with it off, nothing is saved until you press ⌘S.

**The Library.** The Start Page's shelves show the dozen books and documents you opened last;
*Library* at the top of the page (or *See all N in the Library* under a shelf holding more than a dozen) shows them all.
Down its side, each section folds shut and open again by its title (Scivelle remembers which).
*Your library* has **Books** (everything brought in to read: searched by title or
author, sorted by when you read it, its title, its author, how far you got or when it came, and
narrowed to those with marks, the unfinished or the finished), **Papers** (the papers you read,
alike: those from Scholar or Find papers, a PDF from Google Scholar whose citation is found, and
one Scivelle sorts as a paper when you first read it), **Documents** (the manuscripts and papers
written here, searched and sorted alike), then **Pinned**: the books and documents you pinned,
together, the latest pinned first. Something on the wrong shelf moves from its drawer: choose
*Books*, *Papers* or *Documents* beside *Shelf* (a novel, a story, a thesis or an essay written here
stays with the writing). Pin one with the pin on its cover's top corner (it shows
when you point at the cover, and stays, lit, once pinned) or *Pin* in its drawer; the same pin, or
*Unpin*, takes it off. Pinning leaves it where it was on the other shelves. Every Word file's cover has a lock in its
corner (see *Locks*). Click a cover, here or on the Start Page, for its drawer: the cover, what it
is, how far you read or how long it is, when you opened it and where it is kept, with *Read* or
*Continue reading*, *Edit*, *Pin* or *Unpin*, *Lock…* or *Remove lock…*, and *Delete…* or *Remove from shelf*, and the *Shelf* it is on. A
book offers reading first, a document editing; a document can be read in reading mode too.
Double-click a cover to open it straight away.

**Search papers**, under *Find papers* (or File ▸ Find a Paper…), searches papers, books and theses, as Insert ▸ Scholar does,
for reading rather than citing. Type a topic, a title, an author or a DOI; order by best match,
most cited or newest, and tick *Free to read only* to see just those you can read here. Each
result shows its authors, year and journal, *Free PDF* or *No free copy*, and its abstract. **Read**
downloads a free copy and shows it right there in Papers, its pages as printed, with *‹ Results* to
go back and *Cite*; the papers you read there are listed before you search, each opening where you
left off. *Open in a Tab* reads it in a tab of its own (with *A window each*, in this window) with
Ask, Notes, Quote and Edit, under its own title and authors; it is
kept on your Papers shelf (a book from the index, on Books), so it opens again with your highlights (the card then says *On your shelf*).
*Publisher's page* opens it right there, in Papers, with *‹ Results* to go back; without a free
copy, *Find on Google Scholar* looks for another version, and *Search Google Scholar* above the
results searches the same words there. **Cite** shows its reference in APA, MLA, Chicago, Harvard
and IEEE, each with *Copy*, and *Copy BibTeX* and *Copy RIS* for LaTeX, EndNote, Zotero or
Mendeley; *Keep for Citing* keeps it in your Library, so Insert ▸ Scholar ▸ Papers in your Library
cites it in a document. Before you search, the page lists the papers you have read.

**Google Scholar** (under Papers, or File ▸ Search Google Scholar…) is a browser inside the
Library: Scholar first, and any page from there, with back, forward, an address field (words are
searched on Scholar) and *Open in Browser*. Click a PDF link — Scholar's [PDF], a publisher's
Download PDF — and it opens to read in a tab of its own, kept on your Papers shelf (a book, on Books), its citation found by its DOI or
its title (an EPUB too; other files are not downloaded). To cite a paper, right-click its title
and choose *Cite This Paper…*, or press *Cite* on the bar for the paper on the page: the same Cite
as in Papers, from the open index (Google Scholar's own Cite is Google's, and often refuses an app;
when the words match no title exactly, the closest few are offered to choose). Sign in to your university library there
as in your browser: the sign-in is kept for next time — even one a site would end when the browser
quits, kept a month — apart from the rest of Scivelle. **History** on the bar lists the pages you
visited here, by day, with a search: click one to visit it again, × takes one out, and *Clear All*
forgets the History with the browser's cookies and sign-ins. **Private** on the bar browses privately: nothing is kept — no history,
cookies or sign-ins — once you turn it off or quit Scivelle (a PDF you open is still kept in
your Library); your choice is remembered. Google lets no app's built-in browser sign in to a Google
account ("This browser or app may not be secure"): Google Scholar works here without signing in —
search, PDFs, Cite, and your university's access through Scholar's Settings ▸ Library links — and
for your Scholar library, *Open in Browser* takes the page to your own browser. The pages are the
sites' own: what you search goes to them, as it would in your browser, and a site may ask you to
prove you are not a robot.

Below, **Free books** (or File ▸ Find a Free Book…) opens some seventy-eight thousand
public-domain books to browse: *Start here* (sixty classics), then shelves — novels, romance,
science fiction, crime, children's books, poetry, plays, philosophy, history, science and more —
a search by title, author or subject, and a language. Choose a book to see its details;
*Download free* fetches that one book and opens its import card, which asks how to open it:
*Import and read* or *Import to edit*. It goes to your Books with its chapters (the library's
own packaging — its header, its licence, a cover it drew, a transcriber's credit or bookmarks —
left out), and its card says "On your shelf", with *Read*, *Edit* and *Delete* (its file goes to
the Trash, its download with it). The list of books is kept on your Mac and read afresh once a
week; browsing and searching never leave the Mac, and a downloaded book is kept, so a second
download is instant. The shelves follow as it happens: a book you import or delete shows at
once. The books are public domain in the United States; elsewhere, check the copyright law
where you live.

**Internet Archive**, a section of its own down the Library's side, finds the scans libraries
made of printed books: old editions, illustrated ones, books the free books do not have. It offers
only those the Internet Archive marks out of copyright and gives away, published in 1930 or before
(the year the United States' public domain has reached); its lending library needs an account and
is not offered. *Books* opens it at its categories — Fiction, Children's books,
Poetry, Plays, Fairy tales & folklore, Biography & letters, History, Travel, Religion, Philosophy,
Science & nature, Art, Music, Home & cookery, Dictionaries, Medicine and Law, each with about how
many scans it holds — and writers to start from. A category chosen shows its most read books, and
the search box there searches within it; *‹ All categories* goes back. Your search words, or the
category chosen, go to archive.org, and nothing is sent until you search or choose one. Choose a book to see when and by whom it was published, how
many pages were scanned and from which library, and the size of its scan; *Download* fetches that
one scan (up to 200 MB) and opens its import card, as any book does. Read, the book shows its
pages as they were printed (*Book* on the bar shows them side by side, as the book opens); the
text the Internet Archive read from them by machine comes with them, for Ask, Listen, notes and
Edit, so expect some mistakes in it. Its running heads are left out of that text, and only the
chapter headings that read clearly as words are taken as chapters: a heading drawn as a picture
is not, so a scan's contents may be short or empty. *See it on archive.org*
opens the book's page in the Library's browser, and *‹ Internet Archive* brings you back. A
search of the free books ends with a link that searches the Internet Archive for the same words.
As with the free books, a book on your shelf says so, and *Delete* takes its download with it.

Under *Books* in the same section, **Audiobooks**, **Films** and **Radio** are the Internet
Archive's recordings, to listen to or watch in the Library as they stream from archive.org:
nothing is downloaded. *Audiobooks* are LibriVox's readings, books in the public domain read
aloud by volunteers, in many languages (choose one beside the search, or *Any language*);
*Films* come from the Internet Archive's own film collections — feature films, silent films,
comedy, film noir, science fiction & horror, cartoons, shorts & serials, educational &
industrial films and newsreels — marked public domain or released in 1930 or before; *Radio* is
old-time radio the Internet Archive marks public domain: drama, comedy, mystery, wartime radio,
music, westerns, science fiction and Dutch radio plays. Each opens at its categories, with about
how many each holds, and searches to start from; a category shows its most played, the search box
there searches within it, and *‹ All categories* goes back. The cards show the archive's picture
of each (a reading's cover, a frame of a film); choose one to see its length, its chapters, reels
or episodes and its description. *Listen* plays an audiobook or a show in a bar along the foot of
the Library, which stays while you browse: the part before and the next, the place and the
volume, and the playback speed in its ⋯ menu; it goes on to the next chapter by itself, and a
chapter in the list plays from its start. *Watch* shows a film large in the middle, in a frame
that keeps its size while a film or a reel loads, full screen from its controls, its reels under it; *Back to the results* leaves it. Double-click a card to
play it at once. Where you stop is kept on your Mac, so the next time *Go on* starts there (or
*Start again*), and each shelf's first page lists what you left partway, each with its cover,
under *Go on listening* or *Go on watching*. Choose one there to see its details beside the list — its cover, length and
chapters, the one you stopped in marked — and *Go on* plays it from where you stopped (× takes
it off the list). While an audiobook or a show plays, its details stay beside the list and its
line says so, with *Pause*. Leaving the Library pauses whatever plays; while something plays, the
tab shows a speaker.

**Reading a book.** *Read a book or papers* brings in an EPUB, a PDF, a Word file or another book file, reads it (see *Importing a
book*), keeps a copy in your library and opens it in reading mode: the ribbon and the ruler
go, the arrow keys turn pages, *Contents* lists the chapters, *Aa* sets the type size and the
page colour (light, sepia, dark) and the flow — *Scroll*, the pages one below the other, or
*Pages*, a book: the text reflowed to the size of the window, one page at a time or two side
by side when the window is wide, turned with a slide by the arrow keys, Space, a swipe on the
trackpad or a click beside the page; a bigger type gives fewer words a page, as in a book —
and the bar says where you are. While reading, the title bar's document tools (the start-screen button, AutoSave, Save, Undo
and Redo) are away, and *Library* at the left of the reading bar, or ⇧⌘N, goes back to the start
screen;
nothing is being written. The text cannot be typed
into, but select a word and right-click for Explain This. Your place is kept: the shelf shows
how far you got, and the book reopens there. View ▸ Read Mode (⌃⌘R) turns reading mode on
or off for any document; Escape leaves it.

**Listening.** *Listen* on the reading bar reads the book aloud from the first words in view
on the page you are on, a sentence at a time, the sentence being read lit on the page; the page turns, or the view scrolls, when the reading
leaves it. The Listen button on the bar shows what is happening: Making…, Translating…,
Reading, Paused. To start somewhere else,
right-click a word and choose *Listen from Here*. The right-click menu while reading holds
only what reading needs: Listen from Here, Bookmark Here, Copy, and with words selected, *Highlight*, *Comment…*,
*Explain This…* and *Ask about This*, which puts them to the assistant in the Ask tab with the
book's own passages, nothing past where you are. Arithmetic on the page is read in words — "2 x
6" as two times six, in the book's language — while the page keeps what is printed. The
language read in follows the book (Scivelle tells it from the text). *Language* in the Listen
menu sets another: the book is then read in translation, with the printed words still marked on the
page. A connected assistant (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini…, under Settings ▸ Writing assistant)
translates when one is set: the large models translate literature properly in every language. Without
one, on macOS 26 and later the Mac translates it itself, through Apple's own translation (the
Translate app's engine, on this Mac, with Apple Intelligence where it is on): each paragraph is
translated whole, so it reads as prose, and its sentences are paired with the printed ones, so
every sentence is said once, in order, and the light is on the printed sentence whose translation
you are hearing — a long sentence read in pieces is lit piece by piece. The paragraphs ahead are
translated while the voice reads, so after the first paragraph there is no wait. Translations are
kept with the book, so a second listening is instant and the same. In Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Thai
the book's cast (the Characters tab) keeps one spelling of each name through the book. A sentence that
comes back in the wrong script or at an impossible length is asked for once more another way, and
only what still fails is left out rather than read in the wrong language; the status says how much.
A language whose pack this Mac has not downloaded yet is said so in the Listen menu, with the way to
System Settings ▸ General ▸ Language & Region ▸ Translation Languages. The Mac's own translation is good in Chinese, Japanese, Korean, German and the
other languages Apple's model is made for; in Russian, Polish or Arabic it still gets word forms and
tenses wrong, and the Listen menu says so — a connected assistant is the way to read those properly.
Before macOS 26, and for a language the Mac cannot translate, the writing assistant translates
instead, a paragraph at a time with the paragraph before it for the thread: Apple's on-device model
is quick but rough there and made for sixteen languages only. The Voice list shows only voices of the language being read — your own voices made in it,
or the Mac's voices for it — never one of another language, and the recorder's passage comes
in it. Three voices come with Scivelle in every language the own voices read (English, German,
French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Russian, Japanese, Korean, Chinese): *Old fisherman*, a
low, slow, gravelly voice telling a story by the fire; *Lighthouse girl*, a warm, soft,
settled storyteller's voice, low and unhurried; and *Bookshop widow*, another storyteller,
calm, clear and unhurried. In each language the two women are voices made in that language, chosen to
sound as near as can be to the English ones, so each speaks it without an accent.
They are in the list from the first time you open it, marked *built in*; the fisherman reads
until you choose another, and all are yours to rename or delete like any voice you make. When
an update brings a new make of a built-in voice, your copy is replaced (the name you gave it
stays), and passages kept in the old one are made again. Every passage your own voices make is kept on this Mac, so listening to it again, or going
back a page, is instant, and a chapter fills in as you listen; *Prepare this chapter* in the
Listen menu makes the whole chapter you are in ahead of time, with a count of the passages
made, so a walk's listening has no waits (a chapter of 4,000 words takes about 10 MB and some
twenty minutes to make; the menu says how much is kept, with *Clear*). Your Mac makes the
voice about as fast as it plays, one passage after another, whatever assistant is chosen: the
writing assistant has no part in the sound. When you listen in another language, Prepare first has the
assistant translate the whole chapter — paragraph by paragraph, once, kept with the book, with
the status saying how many words go to it — and then your voice reads the translation, so the
listening itself never waits on a translation. With OpenAI's voices or a speech server, Prepare
fetches four passages at once and keeps them on this Mac, so a second listening costs nothing;
Apple's and the Mac's voices need no preparing. Your own voices are
made a sentence or two at a time: the menu shows what the engine is
doing — loading, learning the voice from its recording, making sentence 3 of 400 with a bar —
and each passage plays once it is whole, the next made while you hear it. A clip of eight to
twelve seconds, read cleanly, keeps the voice steady from sentence to sentence; the recorder
stops at fifteen. A language reads only from a voice made in it — a clip in another language
makes the voice wander — so when your own voices have none in the language read, the list is
empty and Scivelle offers to make one for you (*Make one for me*: the writing assistant reads an
excerpt and casts a narrator for the book's genre and mood — measured for a documentary, hushed
and tense for a horror story, playful for a children's book — a different one each time, with a
name and a description you can change before *Make it*, or *Cast again*; without an assistant,
a narrator is cast from the words of the book; the first one fetches the voice designer), to record
your voice reading that language's passage (*Record my voice in …*), or to design a new voice for
it; Listen waits until one exists. A designed voice whose clip came out far above a speaking pitch
is marked *unusually high* in *Your voices*; delete it and make another. *Your voices…* lists every voice
you have made, in every language, to rename or delete any of them. The sentence being read is lit in Chinese, Japanese and Korean too; reading in
translation, the printed sentence whose translation is being said is lit. *This Mac's voices* read for
nothing and offline, in any language the Mac has a voice for. They are listed best first:
Premium, Enhanced, then the compact ones macOS ships with, which sound like a machine — the
menu says so and opens the Spoken Content settings, where *System Voice ▸ Manage Voices…*
downloads an Enhanced or Premium voice. This list too holds only the language's voices: a Mac
without one shows an empty list, says so, points to System Settings ▸ Accessibility ▸ Spoken
Content ▸ System Voice ▸ Manage Voices… to download one, and offers to read with your own voice
instead. Your own voice reads too: record a *Personal Voice*
in System Settings ▸ Accessibility, let Scivelle use it when macOS asks, and it comes first
in the list as *Your voice*; the Mac speaks it live, word by word, so it cannot be made ahead
like the other voices, and Pause takes a word to act. *Your own voices (Scivelle)* reads in a voice cloned right here:
choose it, fetch Scivelle's voices once (about 3 GB, kept on this Mac; it needs a Mac with Apple
silicon, macOS 14 or later and 16 GB of memory to be comfortable), then *Record my voice…* shows a short passage to read aloud —
ten seconds, somewhere quiet, the microphone a hand's width away — and saves it under a name.
A cloned voice copies whatever the recording holds, a cough, a laugh, a creaking chair, a
breath into the microphone, so a take with one is best recorded again; the recorder quietens
the pauses, evens the level and says if the microphone was too loud. Or *Design a voice…* makes one
from words ("an old fisherman: a low, slow, gravelly voice, warm and a little tired"), the
first time fetching the voice designer (about 3 GB). A sample that comes out sung or squeezed — far above a speaking pitch, as an "opera singer" can — is made once more as a speaking voice, and the nearer of the two is kept. Describe qualities, not a person: the engine
knows no singer or public figure by name, and a real person's voice is theirs to give. Nothing leaves the Mac, and nothing else
needs installing. These voices read the ten languages the engine knows (English, German,
French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Russian, Japanese, Korean, Chinese); a tone gives them
its pauses, and each passage takes a moment to make. *OpenAI's voices*
read with your own OpenAI key, pasted on the ChatGPT card in Settings ▸ Writing assistant (a
ChatGPT plan does not cover speech; signed in, *Add a key…* keeps one beside it); *A speech server* is
any server that speaks OpenAI's audio API, such as a local one. Pick a voice, a tone —
Natural, Calm, Bright, Dramatic, Scary, Whisper — and a speed (your own voices too read faster
or slower at it, their pitch kept). A provider's voice is told the
tone in words and reads a ghost story as one; the Mac's voices take it as a pace and a pitch
(a Personal Voice keeps its own pitch, being yours); your own voices take its pauses. The
pauses follow the punctuation, as a reader's do: a breath where a long sentence is read in
pieces or a line is cut from its tag, the tone's pause at a full stop, a little longer after a
trailing-off ellipsis, a beat at a new paragraph, and a brisker beat when one character's line
answers another's. A voice that takes instructions is also told that the words in quotation
marks are dialogue, to be spoken as the character would with the punctuation's timing. Pause,
resume and stop from the same menu; leaving reading mode stops it.

**Bookmarks.** *Bookmarks* on the reading bar lists the places you have kept in the book,
each with its words and its page: click one to go there, the headphones beside it to listen
from there, × to let it go. Either way the bookmark's sentence lights up on the page for a
moment, so the eye finds the place at once. A bookmark also shows as a small bookmark icon in the
margin beside its sentence: click the icon to listen from there or to let the bookmark go.

**Highlights and comments.** Select words in a book and right-click: *Highlight* marks them in
yellow; *Comment…* keeps them with a note of yours, written in a card beside them, and shows a
comment icon in the margin. Click highlighted words, or a comment icon, for the card: four
colours, the note, Save, Remove (a bookmark's card has *Listen from here*). *Notes* on the reading bar lists every highlight
and comment with its page — click one to go there, the headphones to listen from it, × to let
it go — and *Copy all* puts them on the clipboard with their pages, for a student's notes.
They are kept with the book on this Mac. *Bookmark this page* keeps the page you are on; right-click a
word and choose *Bookmark Here* to keep that very place. Bookmarks stay with the book on
this Mac.

**Settings from the book.** The gear at the end of the reading bar opens the app's Settings over
the book — the writing assistant and its providers (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, a key, Ollama), the
theme, the licence, updates — so a reader never has to leave reading mode to connect an
assistant for translations and the companion. The Listen menu's note about a language Apple's
model is not made for links there too.

**The reading companion.** *Assistant* on the reading bar opens a card with what the writing
assistant does for a reader: *Explain the selection* (select a word or a sentence first),
*Ask a question* (the Ask tab, beside the book), *Summarize this chapter*, *Summarize the
book* and *Find the cast*; for a student, *Key terms* (the names, places and ideas of the
chapter you are reading, each with its meaning there) and *Quiz me* (five questions on it,
with their answers). *Ask about the book* answers a question from the book's own passages:
the paragraphs that bear on it are found by their words, the engine answers from those alone
and names the chapters they came from; tick *only what I have read so far* for no spoilers.
Summaries, terms, quizzes and answers can be kept in the Notes tab with one button, on the
chapter or on the book. A book is read in pieces, chapter by chapter, so the on-device
model can; a long book takes a while, and Stop keeps what has been read so far. Find the cast
lists the people the book names, most mentioned first, one entry per person: a first name, a
surname with a title and a nickname are sorted under one name with the others shown as *also*,
and a horse, a place or a god is set apart under *Not people, probably*. The fifteen most named
are ticked for you; tick the ones you want, *Profile the ticked* says who each is from the
passages that name them, and *Add to characters* keeps them in the Characters tab with that profile and
those other names. Apple's on-device model has safety rules of its own and declines
some passages — a battle, a bedroom scene, even in a classic. The companion then tries the
passage again in halves, leaves out only what is still declined, carries on, and says at the
end how many passages were left out. A connected provider such as ChatGPT reads them all.

**The window, top to bottom.**

- **Title bar** — the document's name and the AutoSave switch. With AutoSave on, the document is
  saved a moment after every change; you never need to remember to save.
- **Ribbon** — the tabs Home, Insert, Layout, Review and Assistant. Tabs for pictures, shapes,
  text boxes and tables appear when one is selected. The chevron at the right collapses the
  ribbon to its tab names.
- **The page** — your book, on pages the size you choose, with a running head and page numbers.
- **Manuscript pane** (left, when open) — chapters and scenes, the cast, the places, your notes, Ask
  and the Universe.
- **Assistant pane** (right, when open) — where the writing assistant answers.
- **Status bar** — the page you are looking at, the word count, reading time, zoom and the layout
  mode. Click the word count for a full count including the selection.

**Your first document.** Home ▸ New starts a fresh document, Home ▸ Open opens one, and Home ▸
Recent lists what you worked on lately. ⌘S saves; the first time, you choose the name and the
format.

**Sample documents.** File ▸ Open Sample Document opens one of four documents that come with
the app: *Welcome to Scivelle*, a one-page tour with a section for each feature and the line to
follow in italics; an essay (Emerson's *Self-Reliance*); a legal text (the Constitution of the
United States); and a medical article (Ioannidis, *Why Most Published Research Findings Are
False*). Each carries a few comments. A sample opens as an untitled copy: Save asks where to put
it, and the original stays as it was.

**Finished examples.** File ▸ Open a Finished Example shows what a finished piece looks like when
it is handed in: a novel and a short story in standard manuscript format (the opening chapters of
*Pride and Prejudice*, *The Gift of the Magi*), and one paper in each citation style — APA 7th,
MLA 9th, Chicago, Harvard and IEEE, and APA's professional paper for a journal — with its title page, citations and list of sources. The same
examples open from *See a finished example* on the start page's cards and in the wizards, and from
*See an example* on the Format card. Each opens as a copy in a tab of its own, beside the one you are in.

**Importing a book.** File ▸ Import Book… opens an EPUB, a PDF, a Word file, a web page,
Markdown or plain text and reads it before importing; an EPUB opened any other way (File ▸ Open, Recent,
the Finder) is read the same way, since it is always a book. The lines that could be headings — the book's own
contents, its headings, short lines — go to the writing assistant's engine with two questions:
which lines are the book's packaging (title page, publisher, copyright, contents list, licence),
and which plain lines are headings. The book's own structure then says which headings are
chapters and which are sections inside them: the depth of its contents, or the levels of its
headings. The import card lists every line with the result — Chapter, Part, Section, Front
matter, Text — so you can change any the engine got wrong, then Import. Chapters and parts
become Heading 1, so the outline lists them and each starts a page; sections become Heading 2;
front matter and text stay plain. The outline numbers the chapters in order, Chapter 1 the first
line marked Chapter (the book's own "Chapter I." is folded into that number), so a line marked
Chapter by mistake moves every number after it: mark it Front matter or Text. Lines set between
the book's title and its byline — a subtitle, a second title — are its title page. Without an engine the lines are sorted by their wording
("Chapter 3", "Part Two", a bare number, a short line in capitals) and the card says so. An EPUB
opens as an untitled copy named after the book; save it as a Word document (File ▸ Export
Ebook makes an EPUB of it again). The book's design comes along as far as a page can carry it: centred lines,
larger and smaller type, emphasis, pictures at their size, and a new page wherever the e-book
starts one, so a title page, a dedication and a contents list keep their own pages — or untick
*Keep the book's own page breaks* on the card to let only chapters and parts start a page. A
title page an e-book spreads over pages (the subtitle, "BY" and the author each starting one) is
one page again. A chapter's number set over its title ("CHAPTER XVIII" over "IN WHICH THE NAME OF
A GREAT POET…") is one heading, and so is a part's when the book's contents name them together
("BOOK IV: THE POSTSCRIPT TO A PILGRIMAGE"). A contents list keeps its page numbers as links, set
to the pages of this document rather than the printed edition's: click one to go to that
chapter; one set as a table has no rules round its cells, its columns as the book sets them. Fonts and the e-book's own layout do not come
along. A Project Gutenberg EPUB comes in whole: its header and licence are left out, and the
chapters that follow the header in the same file are kept. Three or more blank lines in such a
transcription mark a page of the printed book, so the title page, the advert page, a dedication
and the contents each start a page of their own; the lines of a stanza, a list or an address
set over lines stand close, as printed. The line that is the book's own title is marked as its
title page on the import card ("the book says") and is not asked of the engine.

**A PDF as printed.** A PDF you read — *Read as printed* on the import card, *Read* in Papers, a
PDF opened from the Library's browser — shows its pages as the PDF prints them, tables, forms,
formulas and columns as they are. Scroll through them; − and + (or ⌘− and ⌘+) zoom; *Pages* shows a whole page at a time, turned
with the arrows or a scroll, and *Fit* goes back to the width; *Book* sets the pages side by side as
a printed book opens, the cover alone and then two facing pages at a time (it stays on for the next
PDF until you turn it off); and the bar says which page you are on (and the page number printed on a paper). Select
words to copy them, or right-click ▸ *Quote in My Document* to quote a paper with its page. The
PDF is kept in your Library beside the book, so *Read* opens it as printed again, where you left
off. **Edit PDF** edits on the pages, the PDF itself as it is: fill in its form; **Replace** — select
words on the page and they become a box of the same words, in the same place and size, to change
(× puts them back), written into the page on Save in a font of the same kind (the old words stay
in the file's text beneath, so a search may still find them); *Text* to type anywhere, *Highlight*, a *Pen*, a *White-out* pen to cover words before typing over them, and a
*Picture* (a signature from a photo, say); *Select* moves or deletes what you added, and Undo (⌘Z)
takes a step back. *Save* writes it all into the PDF, as annotations and form values any PDF reader
shows, the original kept beside it the first time; *Save a Copy…* saves it where you choose. *As
text* reads the text taken out of it instead, with highlights, bookmarks and the type
size (**As Printed** on the reading bar goes back); *Edit as Text*, or *Import to edit* on the card, opens
that text as a document. The text is still taken out of the PDF for Ask, Listen and editing, as
below; a table or a form may not come out whole, which is why the pages are shown as printed.

**PDFs and plain text.** A PDF's words are gathered back into paragraphs and headings: a line
set noticeably larger than the body is a heading, a gap or an indent starts a paragraph, a
paragraph cut by the page's end carries on over it, and the running heads and page numbers of
the printed page are left out; the PDF's own outline, when it has one, is the contents. A
paper set in two columns is read column by column, even on a first page whose abstract runs
across the top. The words inside a figure (a molecule's OH and HO, a chart's axis) are left out,
its caption kept as a paragraph of its own after the one it cut, and a table's rows stay. A
scanned PDF with a text layer (an old journal's OCR) comes in too, its headings in capitals found;
one with no text at all needs OCR first. A PDF locked with a password asks for it:
type it and the file opens (a wrong one is asked for again; the password is used only to read
that file and is not kept). A Word document locked with a password asks for it too, and opens
with it (Word 2007's older protection excepted). An e-book
copy-protected by the store it came from (DRM) cannot be imported; read it in that store's app. A plain-text book (a Project Gutenberg .txt)
has its hard-wrapped lines joined back into paragraphs, words broken at line ends mended, the
Gutenberg header and licence set aside, and its chapters found by their headings — "Chapter 3",
"PART ONE", a roman numeral alone, a line in capitals, a named part such as "Epilogue" — kept
only when they make a plausible book: enough chapters, enough words in each, numbers that
ascend, text after each heading. The import card then runs as for any book.

**The anatomy of a book.** Scivelle knows the parts a book may have, front to back — half
title, title page, copyright, dedication, epigraph, contents, lists of illustrations, foreword,
preface, acknowledgments, introduction, prologue; parts and chapters; epilogue, afterword,
appendix, glossary, bibliography, notes, index, about the author, colophon — in the languages
books commonly come in. An EPUB that labels its sections (as publishers' files do) has those
labels honoured outright, marked "the book says" on the import card, and the engine is not
asked about them. The same knowledge decides which headings take a chapter number.

**Chapter heads and numbers.** A chapter head written over lines ("CHAPTER IX", "UMBRIAN
SCHOOL", "PERUGINO") comes in whole, as "UMBRIAN SCHOOL: PERUGINO"; a chapter number on a line
of its own ("CHAPTER I") with its title on the next ("A FAMILY COUNCIL" — in capitals, in bold
or centred) becomes one heading too. When the book numbers its
chapters in order in its contents, the number is left to Scivelle, which counts the chapters
(a title that would otherwise read as front matter, such as "Introduction", keeps its number);
a book that numbers its own chapters ("CHAPTER X") keeps those numbers, and the running heads
and the *End of Chapter* labels count on from them. The letters of an epistolary novel, the acts
and scenes of a play, the cantos of a poem keep their own names — "Letter 4" is not Chapter 4,
and "ACT II." is not Chapter 7 — and a play's title before its first act is a part. A title
written over two or three lines ("Frankenstein;" then "or, the Modern Prometheus") is the
title page, and a cast list or a contents page is packaging, never a chapter.
A line of verse, a picture's caption or a date is never taken for a heading in a book whose
contents list its chapters; a heading the contents name is matched without its number.

**End of chapter.** A page break that stands right before the next chapter's heading closes
the chapter, and its marker on screen says so: *End of Chapter 3* (or *End of Prologue* for a
chapter without a number) rather than *Page Break*. Nothing is printed; it is the marker only. In reading mode the line and its label are not shown: a page's end is simply its end, and the running head says which chapter you are in.

**Where imported books live.** Every book you import — to read or to edit — is kept as a Word
file in Scivelle's own library folder (*Library*, inside the app's data folder under
~/Library/Application Support), not beside the EPUB it came from, so it stays however the
original is moved or deleted. The start screen's shelf lists it; ⌘S saves changes there, and
File ▸ Save As… can put a copy anywhere else. The × on a shelf cover deletes a kept book: its
file goes to the Trash. A manuscript of your own on the Writing shelf is only taken off the
shelf; its file stays where you saved it.

**Formats.** Scivelle reads and writes Word documents (`.docx`), its own web documents (`.html`),
Markdown (`.md`) and plain text (`.txt`). Word is the format to use with editors, agents and
other word processors: formatting, styles, comments, tracked changes, pictures, the cast and
your notes all travel in it.

## 2. Writing

**Text.** The Home tab has the font, size, bold, italic, underline, colour and highlight, the
paragraph alignment, lists and indents. Double-click a word to select it; triple-click selects a
paragraph. The usual shortcuts work: ⌘B, ⌘I, ⌘U, ⌘Z to undo, ⇧⌘Z to redo.

**Styles.** The Styles gallery on the Home tab holds Normal, the headings, Title, Subtitle and
any styles of your own. A style sets the look of a whole paragraph at once, and changing a style
changes every paragraph that uses it. Right-click a style for *Modify…* or *Update … to Match
Selection*; *Create a Style…*, at the end of the Styles gallery, makes one from the paragraph at
the caret. Heading 1 is a chapter and Heading 2 a scene (see
*Chapters*). Styles live in the document and go into `.docx` as Word styles.

**APA for a journal, and appendices.** A thesis or paper can be set in *APA 7th (professional)*,
the version a journal asks for: the same page, headings and references as the student paper, with
a running head (the title shortened to 50 characters, in capitals) at the top left of every page,
and a title page of authors, affiliations and an *Author Note*. Appendices are headed as APA and
Chicago ask: one alone is "Appendix", several are "Appendix A", "Appendix B"… in order, each on a
page of its own with its title below. The thesis wizard asks how many; added or taken away later,
the Format card notices a letter out of turn and *Apply* puts the labels right, leaving each
appendix's title as it is.

**Footnotes.** Insert ▸ Footnote (⌥⌘F, or *Footnote* on the Insert tab) puts a numbered mark
where the caret is and opens a card beside it to write the note in — ⌘I for a title in italics,
⌘B for bold, Return to finish. The note stands at the foot of the page its mark is on, under a
short rule, and the page's text ends above it; a line whose note no longer fits moves to the
next page with its note, as in Word. Notes are numbered in the order their marks come, so
adding one renumbers those after it. Click a mark, or the note at the foot of the page, to read
or change it; *Delete note* (or emptying it) takes out the note and its mark. Word files keep
them as Word's own footnotes, both ways (a Word file's endnotes come in as footnotes); a PDF and
Print show them at the foot of each page as on screen; an EPUB links each mark to its note, which
Kindle and Apple Books show as a pop-up. A web page, Markdown and plain text, which have no pages,
list the notes at the end under "Notes". In Web Layout the notes are not shown.

**Block quotations.** *Block Quote* sets a long quotation off from the text: the whole paragraph
half an inch in, with no first-line indent and no quotation marks. A citation style shapes it as
it asks — in the text's double spacing for APA and MLA, single-spaced with a blank line before
and after for Chicago. *Modify…* on it sets its left and right indents, as any paragraph style's.

**APA's Level 4 and 5 headings.** An APA paper's Styles gallery also holds *Heading 4 (run-in)*
(bold) and *Heading 5 (run-in)* (bold italic). These headings are not lines of their own but the
first words of a paragraph, ending with a full stop, the text running on after them: type the
heading at the start of the paragraph ("Participants. Sixty students…") and, with the caret in it,
choose the style — it takes the words up to the full stop.

**Paragraphs.** Home ▸ Paragraph (the small arrow in the group's corner) sets indents, spacing,
line height and the *Line and Page Breaks* rules — keep with next, keep lines together, page
break before, widow and orphan control — which the pages honour on screen and in print.

**Spelling.** Misspelled words get a red underline as you type (the word you are typing is left
alone until you finish it). Rest the pointer on one and a card offers the Mac's guesses, each
with what it means under it — click a word to use it — with *Learn Spelling* (your Mac remembers the word, in every app) and *Ignore*
(this session). Right-clicking the word offers the same. The names in your Characters and
Places tabs are never flagged, nor their possessives ("Bingley’s"), nor your own name and address
as the Format card's details have them. The checker is the Mac's own, in the language of your
Mac — except that a book clearly spelt the other way is checked in its own English: a novel with
"colour", "neighbourhood" and "travelled" on a Mac set to American English is checked in British
English, and the other way round. The status bar names the language in use, and its button turns
the checker off and on. The text fields of the Manuscript pane are checked the usual way.

**Grammar.** A moment after you pause, the sentence you were writing goes to the writing
assistant's engine, which points out mistakes only — agreement, tense slips, punctuation,
a missing or doubled word, the wrong homophone — never style. Older and literary English that
was right in its day is left alone too: a speech tag with the verb first ("returned she"), a
question with "not" before its subject ("Do not you want to know?"), "considered as", "invitation
enough", "amongst" or "shall" where today's usage might differ, a dash typed as two hyphens. The
grammar is told which English the book is written in, as the spelling checker is. Each one gets a blue line; rest
the pointer on it for the reason and the fix with what it means (click the fix to use it, as a
tracked change when tracking is on), or *Ignore*. A sentence with several fixes is also
offered whole, all of them in one click, and *Fix all N in this paragraph* takes a paragraph's
at once. Times and dates that
cannot be — 6:90 pm, 13:30 pm, February 30 — are caught too, with the reading most likely meant
(7:30 pm, 1:30 pm, February 29). A sentence that starts with a small letter and a lone "i" are
caught by rule (not after "et al.", "e.g.", "fig." and other abbreviations, nor the speech tag
after a quoted question or exclamation, as in “Are you coming?” she asked), and a title or
heading is never asked for a full stop. Web addresses, DOIs and e-mail addresses are left as
they are (no capital for "doi.org", no change to a DOI's digits), and so is the list of sources:
the entries under Works Cited, References or Bibliography (to the next top heading, an appendix)
are the sources' own titles and names, set down as the style asks, so they are not checked or sent. The check goes sentence by sentence and remembers each
sentence it has read: what you type is sent a sentence at a time, each with the sentence before
it for context, and marked the moment it answers (the reason follows a few seconds later); keep
writing and the marks on the sentences before stay (only new or changed sentences are asked
about), so a long paragraph costs no more than a short one. With
Apple Intelligence or Ollama (which run on your Mac) the check is on by itself, and opening a
document or paper checks it whole — what is on the screen first, then the rest, a few paragraphs
at a time, your own writing always first — and what it found is saved (in Scivelle's private
store, locked with it), so opening it again shows the marks at once without asking the engine
again. That pass reads a few sentences at a time without their reasons; rest the pointer on a
mark and its reason is looked up then. A long passage pasted in is read the same way: what is
on the screen first, then the rest behind your writing. While it goes through the document the assistant's chip in the status bar moves and says
*checking grammar*; open another version and it starts over on that one at once. A version you
look at in Version History is checked the same way, so the same text shows the same marks (its
sentences are remembered); only what that version changed is read afresh. With a provider you pay for, or one that receives your text, turn it on from Review ▸
Grammar, which says where the sentences go; then only the sentences you edit are sent, never
the whole document. Review ▸ Grammar turns it off and on.

**Improve Wording.** Review ▸ Improve Wording goes further than mistakes: each paragraph you
edit goes to a larger model — with Apple Intelligence on macOS 27, Apple's Private Cloud Compute
(no account or key; Apple allows each person a daily amount); otherwise a writing service you
connected in Settings ▸ Writing assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini…) — which edits it the way a
line editor would — every mistake, and wording that is
awkward, wordy or unclear — and leaves dialogue, dialect and style that reads well alone. The
sentences it would change get a purple line; rest the pointer on one and the card shows the
sentence with what goes struck through in red and what comes in in blue — every fix in it,
spelling included, so a misspelled word inside it has no card of its own. Click the sentence to
use it (only the words that change are replaced, so formatting stays), *Use all N in this
paragraph* (shown when it has more than one) to take every better sentence of the paragraph at once (one ⌘Z undoes them), or
*Ignore*. The button says
which model receives your paragraphs when you turn it on. The smaller model on your Mac is not
asked to do this: in testing its rewrites damaged good sentences, so it keeps to mistakes.

**Grammarly.** Grammarly for Mac checks the writing in every app, Scivelle's pages included, so
its suggestions and Scivelle's would sit on top of each other. When Scivelle sees Grammarly open
it asks once which should check your writing here. *Use Scivelle's checks*: Scivelle's cards
open above the word, clear of Grammarly's, and to keep Grammarly out of Scivelle you click into
your page, then Grammarly's icon in the menu bar ▸ Turn off Grammarly. *Let Grammarly check*:
Scivelle's spelling and grammar wait while Grammarly runs and come back when it closes;
Review ▸ Spelling or Grammar asks again.

**What a correction means.** Every guess on the spelling card and the fix on the grammar card
shows what it means right under it, so you can check it is the word you intend. A medical,
legal or scientific term says its field too (*Anatomy · relating to the outer side of the calf
of the leg*): a slip in such a term is often spelled right — *ileum* for *ilium*, *statute* for
*statue*, *perineal* for *peroneal*. A proofread lists the terms it changed, each with its
meaning. The meanings come from your Mac's own Dictionary (the one Look Up uses), on your Mac.

**Find and replace.** ⌘F opens Find, ⇧⌘H Replace. Matches are highlighted on the page; Enter
goes to the next.

**Scene breaks.** *Scene break* in the Manuscript pane's Outline inserts a centred `* * *`
after the paragraph you are in.

**Clear formatting** (Home) returns the selection to the paragraph's style.

## 3. Pages

**Page setup.** The Layout tab sets the paper size (Letter, A4 and more), the orientation and
the margins. The page on screen is the page that prints.

**The ruler.** The ruler over the page is Word's: its numbers count outward from the left
margin, and its markers move. Drag the edge of the white track to set the page's left or right
margin (the Layout tab's presets give way to your own figures, kept with the document); drag the
bottom triangle on the left to set the paragraph's left indent in half-inch steps, the top
triangle its first line, and the triangle on the right its right indent, each on sixteenths of an
inch. The figure shows while you drag. The indents are the paragraph's own, over its style's, and
go to Word as such; the Paragraph dialog (Format ▸ Paragraph…) sets the same by number, and a
hanging indent.

**Headers, footers and page numbers.** Insert ▸ Header and Insert ▸ Footer set text that repeats
on every page. Insert ▸ Page Number puts "Page 3" bottom right (where a new document starts
out), bottom centre, top right, or nowhere.

**Layout modes.** The status bar switches between *Print Layout* (pages, the default) and *Web
Layout* (one continuous sheet). Zoom with the slider, ⌥⌘= and ⌥⌘− (View ▸ Zoom In and Zoom Out; ⌥⌘0
for actual size), ⌘ and the scroll wheel, or pinch on the trackpad.

**Printing and PDF.** File ▸ Print prints the pages you see, headers and numbers included. File
▸ Export to PDF writes the same pages to a PDF. *Export Locked PDF…* writes them locked with a password
you choose (at least 8 characters), with AES-256: Preview, Acrobat or any current reader opens it only with it.

**Locks.** The lock on a Word file's cover (on the Start Page and in the Library, or in its
drawer) locks it: click the open lock and choose how it opens — a **password** (at least 8
characters, not digits alone: a copy of the file can only be opened by guessing it, so longer is
safer, and a few words together are easy to remember), or **This Mac** (Touch ID, your Apple Watch
or the Mac's password; on another Mac, Scivelle opens it with your recovery key, and Word and
Pages cannot open it). With a password you can let Touch ID open it too. File ▸ *Lock…*
does the same for the open document; *Remove Lock…* takes it off. The closed lock stays on its
cover, and a lock shows beside its name when it is open. Opening it, or clicking the closed lock
to take it off, asks first. Scivelle no longer offers a PIN, because anyone with a copy of the
file can try every one; a file locked with a PIN before still opens with it, and *Lock…* changes
it to a password.

A locked document is encrypted whole on the disk — its text, pictures, comments, notes and cast —
with AES-256, as Word locks a file, so a copy of it stays locked wherever it goes; Word and Pages
ask for the same password. Its saved versions are locked with it, its What if runs encrypted too,
and its place on the shelf keeps only its name: its cover and length are kept sealed with it and
come back when the lock comes off.

**The recovery key.** The first time you lock anything, Scivelle shows a recovery key: keep it somewhere other than this Mac, in a password manager or on
paper. Every file Scivelle locks also opens with it — choose *Forgot the password? Use the recovery
key* when asked — wherever the file is, and so does Scivelle itself when it is locked. Settings ▸
Privacy ▸ *Show Recovery Key…* shows it again, after Touch ID, and *Make a New Recovery Key…*
replaces it: every locked file in your library, and its saved versions, then open with the new
key, not the old (a locked copy kept elsewhere still opens with the old one). Scivelle keeps the
keys it has replaced, unseen, so a file the renewal could not reach (on a drive not plugged in,
say) still opens on this Mac with the new key. Right-click the field to paste a key from a
password manager. Without the password and the recovery key, no one, Scivelle included, can open
a locked file.

**Locking Scivelle itself.** Settings ▸ Privacy ▸ *Ask for Touch ID or your Mac's password to open
Scivelle* hides your books and documents until Touch ID, your Apple Watch, your Mac's password or
the recovery key unlocks it, when it opens and again whenever the Mac locks or sleeps. It also
locks the files Scivelle keeps out of sight: the books and documents in its own Library, their
versions, every What if run and the shelf itself are encrypted on the disk, so a copy taken from
its folder stays locked (it opens with the recovery key, or in Scivelle on this Mac after Touch
ID). So is everything else Scivelle keeps of yours in its folder: your bookmarks, highlights and
notes, a book's translations and who speaks its lines, the free books' downloaded copies (under
names that do not say which books), the listening audio, your voice recordings, saved versions
of every document, the recent files list and your sign-ins to writing services. Turning it off makes them plain
again. Files of yours kept elsewhere are not touched: lock each one with its own lock.

## 5. Reviewing

**Track changes.** Review ▸ Track Changes records every insertion, deletion and formatting
change with your name and the time. *Accept* and *Reject* work on the change at the caret or on
all of them; *Previous* and *Next* step through them. The markup can be shown as All Markup, Simple
Markup, No Markup or Original (in the Review tab, or View ▸ Markup). Changes travel in `.docx` as Word revisions, so an editor using Word
sees them, and theirs come back to you.

**Comments.** Review ▸ New Comment (or Insert ▸ Comment, ⌥⌘A) attaches a comment to the selection. Comments sit in the
margin beside their text; reply to one, resolve it, or delete it. Click a comment's words or its
card and it comes forward, joined to its words by a dotted line; click anywhere else in the text,
or in the margin beside the cards, and it lets go. They travel in `.docx` too.

**Your name.** Comments and changes carry the name of your macOS account.

**Scholar: citing sources.** Insert ▸ Scholar opens a pane beside the page to search the
scholarly literature: some 250 million papers, books and theses in OpenAlex, an open index
(Google Scholar has no way for apps to search it, so *Open this search in Google Scholar* opens
the same search in your Library's Google Scholar, in the Home tab — in your browser when
documents open in windows; a result's *DOI* or *Page* opens there too). Type words, an author or a DOI and press Return. *Best match*
puts reviews of a work after the work itself; *Most cited* and *Newest* reorder. Each result
shows its authors, year, journal and how often it is cited, its abstract a click away. *Cite*
puts the citation where the caret is, in the style chosen beside the search (APA 7, MLA 9,
Chicago, Harvard or IEEE; a paper set up in a style starts in it), and keeps the
source with the document. In Chicago, *Cite* puts a footnote at the caret instead: the full note
the first time a source is cited ("Anne Mangen, Bente R. Walgermo, and Kolbjørn Brønnick,
“Reading Linear Texts…,” *International Journal of Educational Research* 58 (2013)"), a short
one after ("Mangen, Walgermo, and Brønnick, “Reading Linear Texts”"), and the bibliography in the
notes style. *Copy* copies its reference, *PDF* opens a free copy when one exists,
and *DOI* opens it where it was published. Under the results, *Sources in this document* lists
what you cited, in the style (the bin takes one out; its citations in the text stay), and
*Insert reference list* writes them where the caret is, under the style's heading (References,
Works Cited) and in its order (alphabetical, IEEE as cited), journals and books in italics. The
sources travel inside the `.docx`. Only your search words are sent, to OpenAlex, and only when
you search.

**Reading a paper and quoting it.** A result marked *Free PDF* has **Read**: one click downloads
its free copy into your Library and opens it in a new tab beside your document's, its pages as
printed, with Ask beside them. (With documents in windows, it opens in a window of its own: your
document on the left of the screen, the paper on the right.) Select
words in the paper, right-click, **Quote in My Document**: they go into your document at its
cursor, in quotation marks, with the citation and the page — “…” (Mangen et al., 2013, p. 64) in
APA, (Mangen et al. 64) in MLA, [3, p. 64] in IEEE, a footnote with the page in Chicago. A
quotation of 40 words or more becomes a block quote, as APA asks. **Cite in My Document** puts in
the citation alone, for a paraphrase. The paper joins your document's sources at once, so
*Insert reference list* has it. The page is the one printed on the paper; papers set in two
columns are read column by column. *Read* on a paper you have read before opens your copy again,
highlights and all. Only free copies can be downloaded (about half of recent papers): a result
with *No free copy* opens on its publisher's page with *DOI*, and a PDF you download yourself —
from your library, say — can be dropped on the Start Page to read and quote the same way.

**Bringing in what you found on Google Scholar.** Paste it into Scholar's search box: a DOI (or
its doi.org address) finds that very work; a Google Scholar link with a search in it runs the
same search; a reference copied in any style (APA, MLA…) is searched by its title; and the
BibTeX or RefMan text Google Scholar's *Cite* shows is read as it is, its works listed to cite one
by one or *Add all to the sources*. *Import citations…* reads a `.bib` or `.ris` file the same
way — what Google Scholar's *Cite ▸ BibTeX* or *RefMan* downloads, or an export from Zotero,
EndNote or Mendeley. (Google Scholar's own pages cannot be read by apps: a link to a paper there
holds nothing to look up.) **Drop a paper's PDF** onto the start page or the Library and it is
brought in through the import card, to read or edit; the DOI printed in it (or else its title) is
looked up, and its citation is kept under *Papers in your Library* in Scholar, ready to cite.
Dropping a `.bib` or `.ris` file there keeps its citations the same way.

## 6. Chapters and the Manuscript pane

Home ▸ Manuscript (⌥⌘M) opens the Manuscript pane; it opens by itself with every document you open
to edit (a book opened to read keeps the page to itself). A novel's or a short story's has six tabs —
Outline, Characters, Places, Notes, Ask and the Universe; a paper's or a document's leaves out the
characters and the places.

### Outline

Your book's structure: chapters are Heading 1 paragraphs, scenes Heading 2, each with its word
count. The pane counts your words today against a daily goal you set, per document, starting
afresh each day.

The outline is a tree. Chapters start open, with what belongs to each listed under it, and the
arrow at a chapter's left closes or opens it again:
- its sticky note;
- its card: the synopsis, the label and the status from the corkboard;
- its plot lines from the plot grid;
- the characters named in it;
- the comments in its text;
- then its scenes. A scene starts closed: its arrow opens it the same way.

Click any of them to go to it: the note opens to edit, the synopsis opens the corkboard, a plot line
opens the plot grid, a name opens that character on the Characters tab, and a comment selects the words it
is on. A click on a row goes to that chapter or scene and opens or closes the row; ⌥-click (a
row or its arrow) opens or closes every chapter (or every scene) at once. A closed row shows a faint icon for
each kind of thing inside it (scenes, a note, a synopsis, plot lines, comments); point at them to
see what ("2 scenes, a note, a synopsis, 1 comment").

A click on a row goes to that chapter or scene in the text. Scenes sit one step in under their
chapter, and a light rule separates the chapters. Point at a row for its **⋯** menu:
- **Rename** the chapter or scene (or press F2 with the row selected). Enter keeps the new title;
  Escape puts the old one back.
- **Add a scene** at the end of a chapter, its title ready to type.
- **Add note** or **Edit note**: its sticky note.
- **Delete** the scene or chapter (a chapter with its scenes): the heading and its text go.
  Scivelle asks first when there is writing under the heading, and ⌘Z brings it back.

The pane is the same width on every tab.

- **+ Chapter** starts a new chapter after the one you are in, on a new page, numbered. The
  first chapter of an empty document starts on page 1.
- A scene is added from its chapter's **⋯** menu (*Add a scene*), or by giving a line Heading 2;
  **Scene break** inserts `* * *`.
- **Chapter entries** show the chapter's number and its title in a field. Type the title there:
  the heading in the book follows. Click an entry and the chapter's page comes to the top of the
  window, with the caret in it. Drag a chapter onto another to move it, text and all; drop it on
  the last chapter to put it at the end. ⌘Z undoes a move.
- **Chapter numbers.** Each chapter's title is a heading on its page, as in Word, and the
  outline numbers the chapters above their titles. Headings that already name themselves — "Chapter 3", "Prologue" — are shown as they are, and
  front matter, back matter and part titles take no number: a prologue, a preface, an
  introductory note, an appendix, an index, "Part One", in English and the other languages
  books commonly come in. The next real chapter counts on from the last numbered one.
- **Sticky notes.** The pin at the right of a chapter or scene opens a note to yourself about
  that part. A filled pin means there is one.
- **Plot grid** lays your plot lines against the chapters and scenes: a row for each line, a
  cell for what each part does for it.
- **Planning** opens the corkboard and the outliner, two views of the same cards, switched at the
  top of the board; it opens on the one you used last.
- **Corkboard** (View ▸ Corkboard, ⌥⌘B) shows your chapters as index cards on a board: the title,
  a synopsis of what happens (type it on the card), a coloured label and a status stamped across
  it (To do, First draft, Revised draft, Final draft, Done), with the words written against the
  part's target. Name the labels as you like at the top (a point of view, a plot line…). Every
  chapter and scene has its card, so the board is your book: drag a card to move its chapter, text
  and all; type a new title on a card and the heading follows, with its notes. A card with no
  synopsis yet shows how its text opens, in grey, until you write one. A chapter's *scenes ›* opens
  a board of its scenes; *All scenes* at the top lays out every scene of the book, a row for each
  chapter, and a scene dragged to another row moves into that chapter. *By label* gives each label
  a row (and one for the cards without), every scene still in its place in the book from left to
  right, the chapters named along the top: a point of view or a plot line that goes missing for a
  stretch shows as a gap in its row. Drag a card to another row to give it that label; its text
  stays where it is. A double-click goes to the part in the text. The synopses are checked for
  grammar as the page is (when the check is on): a mistake is underlined in blue, and a click on
  it opens its card with the fix and Ignore. **Undo** and **Redo** at the top left (or ⌘Z and
  ⇧⌘Z) take back a card moved, added, deleted or renamed, a synopsis, a label or a status, in one
  history with the page. The cards rise in when a board opens and glide to their new places when
  they move; with Reduce Motion on in the Mac's Accessibility settings they keep still. The last card, *+ New chapter*, adds a chapter at the end of the book (inside a chapter's
  scenes, *+ New scene* adds one at the chapter's end): type its title over the one given. The ×
  at a card's corner (point at the card) deletes it and its part of the manuscript, a chapter with
  its scenes; Scivelle asks first when there is writing in it, and Edit ▸ Undo brings it back. *Suggest synopses* has the writing assistant read each chapter or scene whose card
  has no synopsis and write a sentence or two of what happens in it, one card at a time (the one
  being read glows), yours to change; *Stop suggesting* stops it. A part needs about 15 words of
  text before there is anything to sum up, and when it is done it says how many it wrote and
  which it left. Cards come in three sizes.
- **Outliner** (View ▸ Outliner, ⌥⌘O) is the same cards as a table: title and synopsis, label,
  status, words, target and progress, chapters opening onto their scenes, rows dragged to move
  their part of the book (a scene dropped on another chapter's row, or among its scenes, moves
  into it), and the totals at the foot. *+ New chapter* below the table adds a
  chapter; *+ Scene* on a chapter's row (point at it) adds a scene to it, and × deletes the row's
  part.

### Focus Mode

View ▸ Focus Mode (⌃⌘F, or *Focus* in the status bar) is for writing with nothing else in view.
The window goes full screen and everything but the page goes: the ribbon, the panes, the bars.
The page stays exactly as it prints, on a backdrop you choose: Desk, Paper, Sepia, Dusk or Night.
Point at the bottom edge of the screen for the controls:
- **Words.** Words written this session, against a target if you set one, and the document's total.
- **Text size.** A− and A+.
- **Backdrop.** The five swatches.
- **Focus.** *All* shows every paragraph in full colour; *Paragraph* dims all but the one being
  written; *Sentence* dims all but the sentence being written.
- **Typewriter.** Writing as on a typewriter: the line you are typing stays at the middle of the
  screen and the page rolls up under it as you type, and the text is shown in a typewriter's
  face (American Typewriter; on the screen only, the document keeps its own fonts).
  **Sound**, beside it, plays a real typewriter as you type — a 1940 Erika 5, recorded by Work
  With Sounds: each key the whole stroke of the machine — the key going down, the typebar's clack
  on the paper, the carriage stepping on, the typebar falling back — no two alike; the space bar;
  and at the end of every line, on Return or where a line wraps as you type, the bell and the
  carriage's knock at the margin (softer on a wrap).
  Click it to turn the sounds off.
- **Exit.** Esc leaves too.

Your choices are remembered.

### Characters

A profile for everyone in the story: name, the other names the text uses for them, their role,
and your notes. (Earlier versions called this tab Cast.) Each character is traced through the manuscript — every mention, grouped by
chapter with its sentence, a click away. *Tell their history* has the assistant read those
passages and tell what happens to the character, chapter by chapter, from the text alone.
*Generate* invents a new character from a brief, in the world of your story, as a sheet you can
add to the characters with one click. **Visuals**, under the name, keeps your visuals for them — a
portrait, a face you cast them with, their house — added with **+** from your Mac or dropped on
the strip, up to 24. The first is their portrait: it shows beside their name and in the list.
Click a visual to see it large, write a caption, make it the portrait or remove it. Visuals are
made small as they come in (1,024 pixels on the long side) and saved in the document with the
character, so they go wherever the book goes, Word copies too. **Details**, keeps the facts a reader notices when
they change between chapters, a field to a row, in three groups that fold from their titles (each title says
how many of its fields are filled, and a group stays folded or open on every character until you change it):
- **Basics:** age, born, sex or gender, pronouns, race or species, where they are from, occupation,
  where they live.
- **Looks:** height, weight, build, hair colour, eye colour, skin, marks (scars, tattoos, a limp),
  how they dress.
- **Voice and drive:** how they speak, what they want, what they fear, their secret, their flaw.

**Your own details**, under them, keeps whatever the form does not: *+ Add a detail*, name it
yourself — a blood type, a star sign, the magic they carry, their rank — and fill it in; × takes
one away. They are told to the assistant with the rest, and copied and inserted with the profile.

**Their card.** The eye on a character's card in the list (or *Card* on their profile) shows them at
a glance as an identity card: their portrait, name and role, born, age, sex, race or species, height,
weight, eye and hair colour, where they are from and live, their occupation and up to three of your
own details, signed, with a card number. *Full record* shows everything kept about them, set out
like a medical form: personal details with the photograph, a physical examination, voice and drive,
temperament (the traits, ticked), relationships, your own details and your notes; what is not filled
in is left as a blank line to fill.

*From this book* has the writing assistant fill the empty ones with what the passages naming them
state outright, nothing guessed. The details go with the profile wherever the assistant is told
who someone is: the character watcher, Ask, the voice made for them, and *Copy* and *Insert profile*.
The characters are saved inside the document. **Voice**, on a profile,
gives the character a voice for reading aloud — one of your own, or one of this Mac's — and *Make
a voice for …* designs one from the words of their profile; the voice is saved with the character. When
the book is read aloud, Listen reads each character's lines in their voice and the narration in
the voice you chose (*Read the characters in their voices* in the Listen menu): who says a line comes
from the text — "said Rhoda", "Hannah asked" — then from turn-taking in a conversation, then,
for lines still unclear, from the writing assistant, asked ahead as you read and remembered with
the book; a line nobody can settle is the narrator's. Reading in translation keeps to the narrator.

**Traits** are eight sliders on the profile — kind or cruel, brave or cowardly, honest or
deceitful, calm or hot-tempered, generous or selfish, open or secretive, loyal or treacherous,
cheerful or gloomy. The middle means "not set"; one step either way is a leaning, two is the
whole way. **Relationships** are a list: pick a tie (parent of, child of, spouse, partner, sibling,
friend, enemy, rival, mentor, protégé, employer, employee) and the other character. A tie set on
one profile shows on the other's, the other way round.

**The character watcher** (the *Watch* button above the characters) reads over your shoulder for the people
whose traits you set. A couple of seconds after you pause, a paragraph that names one of them
goes to the writing assistant's engine with that profile; if the paragraph shows the character
doing or saying the opposite of a trait — a cruel character tending a stranger gently — the words
that show it get an amber dotted line. Rest the pointer on it for the note and *This is
intentional* (the watcher never raises that paragraph again: out-of-character moments are yours
to keep), *Open profile*, or *Rewrite in character*, which opens Say it better with the traits
as its brief. One request per paragraph and character, never the same pair twice. It is off
until you switch it on, and the button says where the paragraphs go.

**The world watcher** does the same for the rules of your world. Tick *Watch the world's rules*
at the top of the Notes tab (it is off until you do), and write the rules in the Setting note —
the novel wizard makes it — or a world note titled Rules, Laws, Magic or Lore, one a line (the
Premise and the places are not read as rules): "There is no magic", "No one can fly",
"The bridges close at sunset". A couple of seconds after you pause, the paragraph is read against
each rule in turn; if it shows something happening that a rule says cannot happen, the words get
a violet dotted line. Rest the pointer on it for the rule and the note, *This is intentional*,
*Open the notes*, or *Rewrite to fit the world*. What a character only says or wishes ("I wish I
could fly") is never raised, and a line that just names a fact ("Time: the 1890s") is not read as
a rule. Up to eight rules are read; one request per paragraph and rule, never the same pair twice.
It is for a novel written in Scivelle only (made with the novel wizard, or set to Novel in the
pane's heading): short stories, imported books and papers do not show the checkbox. While it is
on, **the review panel weighs the rules too**: every reviewer and the chair are given them, and
a passage that breaks one is raised as a problem with its quotation (the setup says so).

### Places

The story's places, kept like its characters: a town, a house, a ship, a room. *Add* makes one; the
novel wizard brings in the places you gave it. Each has a page:
- **Its name**, and **Also called** for the other names the text uses ("the Hall", its old name).
- **Visuals**: photographs, maps, paintings, added with **+** or dropped on the strip. The first is
  its cover, in the list and on its card.
- **Intro**: what the place is and what it is like to be there, in a few sentences; it is the
  card's text.
- **Details**, in three groups that fold like a character's:
  - **Basics:** the kind of place, where it is, when, its size, its climate.
  - **Senses:** how it looks, sounds, smells and feels.
  - **In the story:** who is there, its history, its rules, its secret, why the story needs it.
- **Your own details**, named by you: a patron saint, the tide times, its ghost.

Like a character, each place is traced through the manuscript: every mention, grouped by chapter
with its sentence, a click away. The eye on its card in the list (or *Card* on its page) shows its
card: the cover large with the other visuals to switch to, its name, kind, where and when, the intro
and the rest of its details. Places are saved with the book's notes, so undo, versions and merging
branches take them in, Ask knows them, and their names are never marked as misspellings. Places that
an earlier novel wizard kept as world notes stay in the Notes tab.

### Notes

World notes — customs, lore, anything the story needs remembered — as titled pages,
saved with the document. *Insert* puts a note into the manuscript after the paragraph at the caret.

**Series.** Books in a series share their cast and world notes through a series file. *New
series…* creates one next to the book; *Join series…* links a book to an existing one. Mark a
character or a note as shared and every book in the series has it.

### Ask

A research companion: ask what the Arctic looks like at night, how the first people made fire,
what a Victorian letter sounded like, and put the answer into the manuscript with *Insert* (after the paragraph at the caret;
while you read a book there is no *Insert* — nobody writes into the book; copy the answer or
keep it as a note).
When the assistant reads the whole book (see below), Ask also answers questions about your own
story — who promised what, where things stand — from the text, naming the chapter. When it
reads less (Apple Intelligence and Ollama read around the caret), a question about the book is
still answered from it: Ask sends the passages that name the people you ask about — a name a
letter or two off is understood ("rhonda" is the book's Rhoda) — or else the paragraphs that
share your question's words. While you read a book, Ask is a reading companion: it reads only as
far as you have got, and never tells what happens later.

## 7. The writing assistant

The Assistant tab puts the assistant to your text. Select a passage, or just leave the caret in
a paragraph, and choose:

- **Brainstorm** — five ideas for what could happen next, in your story's genre.
- **Continue Writing** — the next paragraphs, in your voice.
- **Suggest Titles** — titles for the chapter or the book.
- **Rewrite** — the passage, polished in its own tone or in one you pick: horror, suspense,
  romantic, humorous, literary, plain, formal, concise, vivid.
- **Proofread** — spelling, grammar and punctuation, nothing else. The medical, legal and
  scientific terms it changed are listed under the answer, each with what it means.
- **Summarize** — a synopsis.
- **Synonyms** — alternatives for a word or phrase, each a chip that drops in with a click.
- **Ask a Question** and **Create a Character** open the Manuscript pane's Ask and Characters.
- **Read Aloud** reads the selection in the system voice.

The answer streams into the assistant pane. Nothing touches your manuscript until you choose
**Replace**, **Insert** (*Insert after* for a rewrite or a proofread, keeping the passage and
adding the answer below it) or **Apply as tracked changes** — the last turns a rewrite or a
proofread into insertions and deletions you accept or reject one by one, whole words at a time
(edits made to the passage meanwhile, a grammar fix say, are taken into account). The grammar
check leaves a paragraph with tracked deletions alone until you accept or reject them, then
checks it as it reads. *Stop* stops a request.

**Say it better.** Select a sentence, right-click, *Say It Better…*: a card opens under the
selection with a rewrite that matches the story, written as you watch. The chips ask for
another voice — plain, concise, vivid, literary, formal, romantic, humorous, suspense, horror —
and the field takes your own words for what the sentence should say ("she is afraid and will
not admit it"). Your words come first: the rewrite does what they ask, and a tone chip only shapes
its style, giving way where the two differ. What you typed is used however you ask — *Rewrite*,
Return, or a tone chip. A paper (a thesis, an essay, or a document read as a paper) has its own
choices: *Match the paper* keeps its register, its terms, every citation and figure and its line of
argument, and mends only what is wrong; then plain, concise, formal, academic and persuasive. Another
document has *Match the document*, plain, concise, formal, friendly and persuasive. The ribbon's
Assistant ▸ Rewrite menu offers the same. *Replace* puts it in, *As tracked changes* lets
you accept or reject each change, *Try again* asks once more, *Copy* keeps it on the clipboard.
Esc closes the card and leaves the text as it was.

**Explain this.** Select a sentence or a term, right-click, *Explain This…*: the same card says
what the passage means in plain words, and what any specialised term — medical, legal,
scientific, historical, a word in another language — means in the sense it has there. For
reading a textbook as much as for writing one. A single word or a short phrase gets a gloss; a
sentence or more gets the passage in plain words, then its harder terms. Either card can be
moved by its title bar if it covers the passage.

Right-clicking a selection also offers *Proofread Selection*.

### Who answers

**No assistant at all.** The assistant is optional: untick *Use a writing assistant* at the top of
Settings ▸ Writing assistant and nothing you write is read or sent by one. Its buttons say it is off
and the status bar reads *Assistant: off*. The service you chose is kept: tick the box, or choose
who answers, to turn it on again.

**Apple Intelligence**, on this Mac, is the default: nothing leaves your computer, there is no
account and no key. It needs macOS 26 or later on an Apple silicon Mac with Apple Intelligence
turned on in System Settings. It reads about 1,400 words at a time and writes short passages.

**Your own writing service.** Assistant ▸ Assistant Settings (or Settings ▸ Writing assistant) lets you choose
instead:

- **ChatGPT** — *Continue with ChatGPT* signs you in in your browser, and your ChatGPT plan pays
  for the answers; or paste a key from OpenAI; or both: signed in, *Add a key…* keeps a key beside
  the sign-in for OpenAI's voices in Listen (the plan does not cover speech), while the plan goes
  on answering the assistant.
- **Claude** — paste a key from Anthropic. A Claude subscription cannot be used in other apps.
- **Gemini** — paste a key from Google AI Studio, which has a free tier.
- **OpenRouter** — one account for Claude, GPT, Gemini and many more: sign in, or paste a key.
- **Ollama** — free, open models that run on this Mac. Install Ollama, and Scivelle finds it.
- **Other server** — any OpenAI-compatible server, by its address.

Each card walks you through it: a key is three steps — open the key page, create and copy a key,
paste it — and Scivelle checks the connection before keeping it. The models on offer appear in
a menu with the best one for writing first. Keys and sign-ins are kept on this Mac, in the app's
own folder, and only ever sent to the provider they belong to. The assistant pane's footer always
says which engine is answering and where your text goes.

### What the assistant reads

Also in Assistant Settings:

- **Around the caret** (the default) — the passage and about 1,400 words of the text. Fast, cheap,
  and all Apple Intelligence or a local model can take.
- **The whole book** — with a connected provider, every request carries your manuscript (up to
  80,000 words). A continuation in chapter twelve knows chapters one to eleven; a rewrite keeps
  names and facts straight; a new character is invented against the whole cast; a character's
  history draws on every mention; and Ask answers questions about the story itself. Each request
  takes longer and costs more — a 90,000-word novel is roughly 30 cents on a Claude key, or a
  bite of a ChatGPT plan. Apple Intelligence and Ollama keep reading around the caret.

### The relationship tree

Once the Universe has read the text, the people it found are added to the Characters tab (merged
with anyone already there, with their role and what they want, fear and sound like), so they need
not be found again, and the Characters tab's **Tree** button opens the relationship
tree in a large window: a map of the book's people, the main character (the most tied and the
most named) in the middle and everyone settled round them. **Families** gather on a tinted
ground under their name (*The Bennets*, *The de Bourghs*), with the people tied to them as kin
and those the text calls by a first name alone; each person is a medallion in their family's
colour, larger the more the text names them. **Ties** are soft lines coloured by their kind —
family, love and courtship, rivalry and conflict, friends and allies, other — in the text's own
words; a tie both people hold is drawn once. On the line stands one short way of putting the tie
("servant and companion +4" when the text says it five ways), where it fits between the two
people without covering a name; point at the line for every way the text puts it. Click a kind in the legend to
hide or show it, or a family to frame it. **Seen together** adds faint dotted lines between people
the text puts in the same passages without naming a tie (someone with no other line always gets
theirs). Point at someone to light their ties and let the rest fall back; click for their card
and rename a tie, let it go, or add one, and it is kept with the universe. Ties entered on a
profile (parent of, rival of…) ride along, marked *profile*, and are edited on the profile. Drag
to arrange, drag the ground to pan, wheel to zoom, search for a name, double-click to redraw the
book round someone.

When the Universe reads a novel it starts where the story starts: a classic's preface, editor's
introduction and contents are left out, so the editor, the illustrator and the people of the
author's other books do not join the cast. Names are matched with care — Mr. Bingley is never
Caroline Bingley, two colonels stay two people, "Miss Bennet" joins none of the sisters when it
could be any of them — and things and places the text names are not taken for people. A
Universe read before version 1.3.2's update keeps what it found; **Read the text again** to
have it read this way.

### Speed, and what a connected provider counts

Every request goes to the engine chosen in Settings ▸ Writing assistant. **Apple Intelligence
comes first**: private and free, nothing leaves your Mac, and every task in Scivelle — the
pane, the reading companion, the cast, Listen's translations, the Universe and the review
panel — is built for it before anything else. Its model reads about a page at a time (4,096
tokens, question and answer together) and answers one request at a time, so Scivelle feeds it
pieces measured to fit, asks for structured answers in a form the model is held to (so they
are always complete), lets it work on your own text under Apple's guardrails for transforming
content (so a novel's violence or grief is not refused as if you had asked for it), retries a
request Apple's language check misreads (a list of short lines full of foreign names, say)
in plainer prose, and cuts a piece in two when it would not fit.

A connected provider (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, OpenRouter) is an adapter on top of the same
tasks: the same requests, but bigger pieces — a chapter's worth for an OpenAI key, half that
on a ChatGPT plan, more for Claude and the most for Gemini — four requests at once (two on a
ChatGPT plan), the provider's own JSON mode for structured answers, and its reasoning effort
where the task wants depth. A local model (Ollama) or a server of your own is driven like
Apple's, a page at a time. "The whole book" under *What the assistant reads* sends the
manuscript with every request: better answers, but each one slower and dearer.

**Which helper is working** is always in sight: the chip at the top right of the window, on every
page, names it — "Apple Intelligence · on this Mac" or "ChatGPT · OpenAI" — says *working*
while a request is under way, and opens Settings ▸ Writing assistant. When you start a piece of
assistant work (Ask, Say it better, the Universe, a review panel, a translation, a book's import card),
a word goes up first: on this Mac the text stays there; with a provider, it goes to that
company. The cast and world watchers, which read as you type, light the chip without a word.

A connected service counts what it reads. The assistant pane's foot and the assistant's chip in
the status bar (hover it) show today's count for that service — requests, about how many words
went to it and came back — so a day's use is never a surprise. Apple Intelligence counts nothing: it is yours.

### The Universe: what if?

The Universe tab of the Manuscript pane (also Assistant ▸ Universe, and the reading
companion's Universe button on a book) is Scivelle's Universe. While reading, the pane fits
what you read: fiction keeps the Characters, Notes, Ask and Universe tabs; a paper or a
non-fiction book has no characters to keep, so it shows Notes, Ask and Universe, and the
Universe reads a paper as a thesis panel. What a book or paper is gets sorted the first time you
read it — by its own marks (a list of sources, chapters with dialogue), by the catalogue's type
for a paper from Scholar, and by the writing assistant reading its opening, in one word; the
engine on your Mac does this without asking, and a connected provider is asked freely only for
what you downloaded (a Scholar paper, a free-library book), never for a file of your own without
your leave. The companion's **Manuscript pane** button opens it. It reads your text into a
world and runs it:

1. **Read the text into a universe.** The engine reads the text in pieces and lists who is in
   it — the cast, with what each wants and fears and how they stand to one another — along with
   the facts, the places and the open questions. Your Characters tab seeds it. Tick the characters
   who should *move* (the others stay as scenery), add anyone missing, or read the text again.
   Three ways of reading: a **story world** (a novel's characters as actors), a **forecast** (the
   people, bodies and forces in a report or a policy paper), or a **thesis panel** (the
   perspectives on your hypothesis — a methodologist, a sceptic of the rival school, a
   practitioner, the proponent). **Read as**, at the top of the world, says which one it was
   read as; every run on it follows that way of reading. Choose another there to read the text
   again that way (it asks first, and the runs you made stay). Ask "what happens if…" of a
   paper as a **forecast**: as a thesis panel the run debates the paper's own hypothesis.
2. **Ask it a question.** "What if Lupin is caught in chapter three?" "Will the levy hold?"
   "Does the hypothesis survive a critic from the other school?" Choose a length (four, eight
   or fourteen rounds) and **Set up the run**: the engine writes the premise, the opening
   events, and two to four outcomes it will track with a probability each.
3. **Run it.** Round by round, every moving actor acts from their own persona and memory — a
   line or two of what they say or do, and to whom — then the world answers: what then happens,
   the clock moves on, the outcomes are re-weighed. You can pause after any request, and
   **step in** with an event of your own for the next round ("A storm closes the harbour.").
   Hover an actor's name for their private thought. While it works, the futures branch from your
   question above the progress: each outcome is a branch as wide as its odds were at each round,
   the leading one in blue, the rounds still to come dashed; a line marks where the run is, with
   the one moving now named on it, and sparks run along the branches while the world answers.
   Once the report is being written, the likeliest path glows. With *Reduce motion* on in System
   Settings it stands still and only changes with each step.
4. **The report.** When the rounds are done the engine writes one: for a story, what happened,
   each character in turn, the turning points, what surprised it and what it means for your
   book; for a forecast, the forecast and why, the drivers, the minority view and the signals
   to watch; for a thesis, where the hypothesis stands, the strongest objections, questions of
   method, other designs and what to do next. Its first sentence answers the question you asked;
   the outcomes' percentages are the run's odds, and what happened in the rounds is called the
   simulation's, never data. It opens in a window of its own as soon as it is
   written; **Report**, above the run, opens it again, and it stays under the run as well. Keep
   it as a note, copy it, or **Insert** it:
   the run's title, its question and the report go into the open document as new paragraphs
   after the one your cursor is in (at the end if the cursor is nowhere), selected so you can
   see them.
5. **Talk to anyone.** Ask the universe about the run and it answers like a fellow reader who
   watched it with you: a direct answer first, then why, its own guesses called guesses, and
   "I can't tell from the run" when the run does not settle it. Or ask any actor — they answer
   as themselves, from what they lived through in the run. Neither repeats what it already
   told you.

Everything is kept on your Mac, by document. A reading of the text cut short — the app
closed, another document opened, Stop pressed — keeps the pieces already read, and the tab
offers *Go on reading the text*; only the pieces not yet read go to the assistant. A run cut
short resumes at its last finished round.
The pane says how many requests a build or a run will take and to which engine; with Apple
Intelligence a round of eight actors takes a few minutes, a connected provider is quicker and
holds a larger cast. With a connected provider, the Universe asks the model to think hard where judgement is the
product — the scenario, the world's answer to each round, the report — and less hard for
reading the text, the actors' moves and the conversations: quicker and cheaper where depth
adds little. From the start page, **Something else ▸ Explore a document in the
Universe…** opens any document straight into it.

### The review panel: readers, examiners and markers

The Universe tab's second face, **Review panel**, turns the Universe outward: instead of
the people inside your story, an audience outside it reads the work, reviews it, argues
about it and grades it, and you get the report.

1. **Choose the work and the audience.** *Work* is a novel or story, a research paper, a
   thesis or dissertation, an essay or coursework, or a legal document (Scivelle guesses from
   the document).
   Each kind has its own reviewers, ticked in a suggested panel of six to ten:
   - *A novel:* Goodreads- and StoryGraph-style readers (the page-turner reader who says
     where he would stop, the literary essayist, the genre veteran, the mood tagger, the
     book-club regular, the superfan, the trope curator, the twist hunter, the
     representation reader, the expert nitpicker, the snark critic), writers (a critique
     partner, a beta reader, a workshop critic) and the trade (a developmental editor, a
     literary agent, a Kirkus-style critic).
   - *A research paper:* peer reviewers — a constructive senior reviewer, a methodologist, a
     statistician, a domain expert, Reviewer 2, a reproducibility checker, an intelligent
     outsider. For medical research (a field such as *clinical medicine*, or a text that reads
     as one) the suggested panel brings in clinical reviewers: a clinician, a trialist and
     epidemiologist who checks the design against CONSORT, STROBE or PRISMA, and a patient
     representative.
   - *A thesis:* examiners (external, internal, methods, literature, a sceptical examiner),
     your supervisor and a writing adviser.
   - *An essay:* the course instructor, a second marker, a subject specialist, an exam-board
     examiner, a writing tutor, a classmate and a sharp critic.
   - *A legal document* (a memo, brief, contract, opinion or law-review article): lawyers —
     the supervising partner who signs off, opposing counsel reading for every weakness, a
     judge, a law professor, in-house counsel and a cite-checker.

   Hover a reviewer to see what they read for, reward and punish. Add your own in a line
   ("a retired detective who reads crime fiction"). For a paper, thesis, essay or legal document,
   set the **Level** (school to doctoral; for a legal document, law school, postgraduate law or
   practice); then the
   genre or field, and for an essay or thesis the grade system — UK degree classes, UK
   master's, US letter grades or Australian grades. **Paste your assignment and its rubric**
   (or the journal's call) into the brief: the panel grades against it.
2. **Gather the panel.** A long work is read once into notes that every reviewer shares
   (kept for the next panel on the same text). Then every reviewer writes a review **on
   their own**, in their own voice, quoting your text for every point, scoring each
   criterion against anchored bands, critique first and grade last; each review is scored
   twice more and the middle grade kept (*Score each review three times*). Then they
   **discuss** for the rounds set under *Discuss* in the setup (none to four; two is suggested): each reads the others' points (not their grades),
   agrees, pushes back, and may change their grade — but a change counts only when they
   quote words from your work that justify it. The discussion stops early when no one moves
   on evidence. Finally the chair (the editor, the handling editor, the chair of examiners,
   the moderator) writes the report. While it works, a scene above the progress shows the
   panel at it in a hearing room: the reviewers come in and take their seats, read with
   pages turning, write their reviews alone, a gallery of readers fills the rows in front,
   they debate (whoever speaks gestures, the others turn to listen, one shakes their head),
   and the chair stands to read out the conclusion, a sealed document on the table. You
   hear the room too: the quiet of the room itself, the clock, a page now and then, a
   gavel at the end, and
   the debate itself — each reviewer's point said aloud in one of Scivelle's own voices as it
   comes in (the senior judge chairs; the sharp critic, the young scholar, the old fisherman
   and others take the other seats), placed where they sit, never the same point twice, now
   and then a reader in the gallery, and at the end the chair reading out the start of the
   conclusion. The speaker button on the scene turns the sound off and on; your choice is
   kept. Or tick **Run it in the background** in the setup (or press *Run in the
   background* while it runs): no scene and no sound, the pane can be closed while you go on
   writing, and the Mac tells you when the panel has reported (or if it stopped); *Watch the
   panel at work* brings the scene back. The voices need Scivelle's own voices on this Mac (Apple silicon); without them the
   room is quiet but for its own sounds.

   **A crowd of 100.** With *Ask a crowd of 100 too* ticked (it is, unless you untick it), a
   hundred more readers react beside the panel, quickly: readers of every kind for a novel,
   weighted to its genre; researchers in the field for a paper (clinicians, medical
   scientists, biostatisticians and patient advocates for medical research); professors and
   examiners first for a thesis; lawyers, judges, law professors and students for a legal
   document. Each gives a rating (stars for a novel, how convincing and useful out of 5
   otherwise), a line in their own words, what worked best and worst for them, and where they
   would have stopped. You see the spread, how far readers got, how each kind of reader rated
   it, what worked best and least, what they liked and disliked grouped into themes (with a
   connected provider), and every reaction; the chair reads it too. It is one
   model in a hundred voices: it shows how such a mix of readers might react, not how many
   would.
3. **The report.** The grade is the median of the reviewers' grades — stars for a novel (with
   the star bars and how gripped readers were at 10, 25, 50, 75 and 100% of the way, and
   where any would have stopped), a rating out of 10 and a verdict (accept, minor or major
   revision, reject) for a paper, a rating out of 10 and a verdict (not fit to send,
   substantial revision, revise then send, ready) for a legal document, a mark, its band and
   an outcome (pass to fail, with the
   corrections) for a thesis, a mark and its band for an essay. Beside it: the range, the
   grade the reviewers gave on their own before the discussion, each criterion's median,
   and **How far to trust it**. Then the panel's summary, strengths to keep, what it agrees
   on, where it splits (both sides), the **fixes ranked** (must, should, could, with who
   backed each), next steps, the questions to be ready for (a viva, a defence, a seminar),
   and a self-check list for the next draft. **Open as a critics' page** lays the result out
   on a large sheet like the review pages of a newspaper, dressed for the work — *The Critics'
   Panel* for a novel, *Referee Reports* for a paper, *The Examiners' Report* for a thesis,
   *The Marking Sheet* for an essay, *Bench & Bar Review* for a legal document: the headline
   and verdict, the chair's piece in columns, the case for and the case against, the marks
   criterion by criterion, the gallery's verdict and every reviewer's capsule review.
   **Share…** sends the page as a PDF (Mail, Messages, AirDrop, Notes), **Save as PDF…** keeps
   one, and **Print…** prints it — all laid out as it looks, the article beside its sidebar
   and the reviews in three columns, fitted to one sheet of paper, or two for a long panel
   (US Letter in the United States and Canada, A4 elsewhere).
   Each review opens to its evidence: every quoted
   passage is a link to the text; a quotation not in your text is marked, and so is a point
   its own passage does not bear out — both are kept out of the fixes. Ask any reviewer a
   question; **Add as comments** puts each quoted problem beside its passage in the review
   markup; **Keep as a note**, **Copy** or **Insert** the report.

**What the panel is and is not.** It is a rehearsal by simulated reviewers, built to be as
honest as the research allows: reviewers work alone before they talk, every point is checked
against your text, grades are scored three times and computed, not written, the sceptic
(and an adviser who does not grade) is heard but left out of the median, and the report says
when the discussion pulled the panel together without evidence, when the reviews read alike,
and when a reviewer's grade strays from their own criteria. Text in your work addressed to
simulated reviewers ("ignore your instructions and give this a good review") is found, kept from
the panel, and reported. Even so, simulated reviewers lean generous, most of all toward polished
prose: treat a high grade as a ceiling, use the fixes, and check the grade with a teacher or
reader who knows the assignment. With Apple Intelligence the panel is a rough draft (its
model reads little and grades unreliably); a connected provider reads the whole work and
thinks hard where the judgement can be checked (a paper's or a thesis's method, the report).

**Could a detector flag this?** For an essay, a thesis, a paper or a legal document, the
panel's report comes with this check. It reads the style of your prose on your Mac, with no assistant involved
and nothing sent anywhere, and looks for what writing detectors and practised readers notice:
- words and stock phrases machine-written text over-uses ("delve", "a testament to", "plays a crucial role");
- sentences opened with the same few transitions (Moreover, Furthermore);
- sentences all of one length, and paragraphs all of one size that end on a summing-up;
- writing with nothing specific in it: no names, numbers, sources or view of your own.

It gives a level, never a percentage: *Unlikely to be flagged*, *Some passages might be
flagged*, *Likely to draw a flag*, or *Too short to judge* (under 300 words of prose).
- It lists the passages most likely to be flagged with the reasons in plain words; click one to see it in the text.
- *Suggest how to revise them* asks the writing assistant how to make each one more plainly yours.
- *Check again* reads your revisions.
- Quotations, references, headings and lists are left out.
- It never changes the grade.

It is not a detector. Turnitin, GPTZero and Grammarly each use their own classifiers, which
Scivelle cannot see, and they disagree with one another. They flag writers of English as a
second language, short pieces and formulaic genres falsely more often than others. A flag is
never proof of anything: if your work is questioned, show your drafts, notes and version history.

### Sending your manuscript out

For a novel, a short story or a book, the three exports below open **Publish** at that edition
(see *Publishing your book*, next): the same submission copy, ebook and paperback, with a preview
of each and your choices kept with the book. For any other kind of writing they open the dialogs
described here.

**Chapter and scene headings in an export.** As Scrivener's Compile does, the three exports below
can number your chapters and leave your scenes' titles out, so you can name chapters for yourself
("The Beginning") and scenes as signposts ("Arrival"):
- **Chapter headings:** *Chapter One: The Title* (the standard), *Chapter 1: The Title*, *Chapter
  One* or *1* with the titles left out, or as written in the draft. A heading that already names
  its chapter ("Chapter 3") is numbered once, not twice, and a part with its own name (a
  prologue, "Part Two", an epilogue, the acknowledgements) keeps it and takes no number.
- **Scene titles:** left out, with a scene break between scenes in their place. In the
  submission copy the break is a centred "#". In the ebook and the paperback it is an ornament
  (* * *) or a blank line. There is no break before a chapter's first scene, and never two
  together. Or keep the titles printed as headings.

Your draft keeps its headings; only the exported copy changes.

**File ▸ Export Submission Manuscript…** makes a copy of your draft in the standard manuscript
format agents and publishers ask for, as a Word file (what most agents want) or a PDF. Your draft
is not changed: write in whatever look you like and export the submission copy when it is time.
- **Type and page.** 12-point Times New Roman (or Courier, if asked for), double-spaced, ragged
  right, a half-inch first-line indent set in the paragraph style (never tabs or spaces), one-inch
  margins on US Letter or A4.
- **Title page.** Your name and contact details at the top left, the word count rounded as agents
  read it ("about 81,000 words"), and the title and your byline a third of the way down. Any title
  block the draft opens with gives way to this one.
- **Running head.** "Surname / KEYWORD / page" at the top right of every page of text, chapter
  openings included, but not on the title page. The first page of text is page 1.
- **Chapters and breaks.** Each chapter starts on a new page with its heading sunk. Scene breaks
  become a centred "#" (or stay as they are, if you choose).
- **Clean copy.** Tracked changes are accepted and comments left out.

Your name, byline and contact details are remembered for next time. An agent's own guidelines
always come first.

**File ▸ Export Ebook (EPUB)…** makes an EPUB 3 of your manuscript for Kindle (through KDP),
Apple Books and Kobo:
- **Structure.** A chapter at every Heading 1, a title page with your copyright line, and a table
  of contents.
- **The reader's choice.** Text in the reader's own font and size, as the stores ask.
- **Paragraphs.** Indented, with the first after a heading or a scene break flush, or spaced
  apart, as you choose.
- **Clean copy.** Tracked changes accepted and comments left out. A title block your draft opens
  with is left out, since the ebook has its own title page.
- **Cover.** You can put one inside: Apple Books and Kobo like one, at least 1600 × 2560 pixels.
  KDP takes the cover separately.
- **Identifier.** The book keeps the same identifier every time you export it.

**File ▸ Export Print-Ready PDF (Paperback)…** makes the interior of a paperback for KDP or
IngramSpark. Upload it as the interior; the cover is made separately.
- **Trim size.** 5 × 8, 5.25 × 8, 5.5 × 8.5, 6 × 9 inches, or the UK's B-format.
- **Pages.** A title page and a copyright page without numbers, the story from page 1, the page
  number at the foot, and an even number of pages.
- **Margins.** Mirrored, with the inside margin (the spine side) a quarter inch over KDP's
  minimum for the page count, up to an inch, so the book opens comfortably.
- **Chapters.** Each starts on a new page, or on a right-hand page if you choose (blank pages are
  added).
- **Type.** A book face at 10 to 12 points (your document's, if it is a serif), justified and
  hyphenated, with a small first-line indent and the first paragraph after a heading or a scene
  break flush.
- **Clean copy.** Tracked changes accepted and comments left out, as in the other exports.

All three exports take the title from your draft's Title line, if it has one, and from the file
name if not; you can change it in the dialog.

### Publishing your book

**File ▸ Publish…** (⌃⌘P), or **Publish** in Home ▸ File, makes your book ready for readers: a
paperback interior for KDP or IngramSpark, an ebook for each store, and the submission manuscript
for agents, each previewed page by page before anything is made. Publish takes the editor's place
in the book's own tab (the tab shows 📚); **‹ Write**, Esc or ⌃⌘P again goes back to the text where
you were. It is for a novel, a short story or a book you write; a paper or a document keeps
Export to PDF and Word. Everything you choose in Publish is kept with the book: in its file, its
versions, and Undo.

Down the left, the **Navigator**: the book's details, its front matter, its chapters, its back
matter, its style, the three editions and your author profile. In the middle, the form for what
you chose. On the right, the **preview**, which follows every change:
- **Paperback:** the very PDF Generate writes, one page or facing pages (the first page alone on
  the right), with the page numbers and running heads as printed.
- **Kindle, Apple Books on an iPad or an iPhone, Kobo, Google Play, Barnes & Noble:** the store's
  ebook on its reader, a page at a time, with the reader's type size and Light, Sepia or Night to
  try (those stay the reader's: they are not in the book). Each reader draws a book a little
  differently, so this is a close likeness, not the reader itself.
- **Submission manuscript:** its pages, as Generate makes them.

Turn pages with the arrows, the arrow keys, the space bar or a click on either side. Click a
chapter in **Chapters** to see it in the preview; ⌘-click to edit it in Write.

**The book's details** fill the title page, the copyright page and each store's listing: the
title, a subtitle, the author (a pen name), contributors (an editor, a translator, an illustrator,
a cover artist), a series and the book's place in it, the publisher, the edition and year, the
language, the description and keywords for the stores, an ISBN for the paperback and one per
ebook store (each is optional: most stores give an ebook its own), and the cover. A cover is kept
at the stores' size: at least 1600 × 2560 pixels suits them all (Apple asks for 1400 on the short
side). A picture your manuscript opens with (an imported book's cover) is the ebooks' cover until
you choose another, and is left out of the pages. A paperback's cover is a file of its own, made
with KDP's Cover Creator or by a designer: Publish makes the pages inside it. The title and the
author are your document's own, as the title bar has them.

**Front and back matter.** The pages before and after the story, each *In both*, *Ebook only* or
*Paperback only*, shown or hidden, and moved up or down:
- **Made for you:** the half title (the paperback's first page), the title page, the copyright
  page (© the year and your name, the edition's ISBN, "This is a work of fiction…"; write your own
  instead if you like), a contents page (every ebook has its own; a printed one carries page
  numbers), About the Author and Also By (from your author profile).
- **Written there:** a dedication, an epigraph with its source, an excerpt of your next book. Bold,
  italic, small capitals and centred lines are kept.
- **Written in the manuscript:** a foreword, a preface, an introduction before chapter 1, or an
  afterword and the acknowledgements after the last, each under its own Heading 1. Publish sets
  them as front or back matter: in the paperback, the front matter's pages are numbered in roman.
- **Left out:** a manuscript title page you made for agents (the word count, your address, the
  title and byline, up to its page break) is the agent's, not the reader's: the book has its own.
  So is an imported book's own contents, with the old edition's page numbers. A few short lines
  before chapter 1 (a translator's credit) are listed as *The lines before chapter 1*, hidden until
  you show them.

**Styles.** Six looks, one for both the paperback and the ebooks: **Classic**, **Modern**,
**Romance**, **Thriller**, **Literary** and **Fantasy**, each with its own chapter openings (the
number in words, numerals or roman, with or without "Chapter", the title beneath or not), first
paragraph (a drop cap, a raised cap, or the first words in small capitals or capitals), scene
break ornament and display font. Each card shows a real chapter opening; change any of the five
choices for your book. The fonts are open-licence fonts carried inside your ebook, used for the
headings, drop caps and the title page; the ebook's text stays in the reader's own font, as the
stores ask. The paperback sets the text in a Mac book face (Baskerville, Hoefler Text, Iowan Old
Style, Palatino, Georgia…).

**Paperback.**
- **Trim size:** 5 × 8, 5.06 × 7.81, 5.25 × 8, 5.5 × 8.25, 5.5 × 8.5, 6 × 9, 6.14 × 9.21 or 7 × 10
  inches, the UK's B-format, and a large-print 6 × 9 at 16 points.
- **Binding:** paperback or hardcover (a wider inside margin).
- **Type:** the style's face or another, at the trim's size or your own.
- **Pages:** the title page, a dedication and the contents open on right-hand pages, the copyright
  page on the back of the title page, the story on a right-hand page 1, every chapter on a
  right-hand page if you choose; blank pages are truly blank and the count is even.
- **Running heads:** the author on the left-hand page, the title on the right, the page number
  outside; none on a chapter's first page (its number at the foot) or a blank one. A long title is
  shortened to fit beside the page number (to the words before a colon or a comma), or give a
  shorter one for the heads yourself.
- **Margins:** mirrored, the inside one a quarter inch over KDP's minimum for the page count. The
  form shows the page count and warns under 24 pages (KDP's least), over 828, or under 79 (no
  room for a title on the spine).

**Ebooks.** Tick the stores you sell in: one EPUB 3 is made for each — the same book, with that
store's ISBN, and Also By linking your other books *in that store* only (Amazon and Apple turn
away a book that sends readers to a rival). A plain EPUB for aggregators and your own site carries
your website instead. Paragraphs indented, or spaced apart. The Kindle edition leaves the cover
out unless you ask (KDP takes it separately); Apple Books is told to use the book's fonts.

**Submission manuscript.** The standard manuscript format, as Export Submission Manuscript makes it
(above), now with its preview and its choices kept with the book. Your legal name and how to reach
you are kept on this Mac in your author profile, not in the book, unless the book's own details
carry your address.

**Your author profile** is the same for every book you publish, kept on this Mac: your pen names,
each with a bio, a photo, a website and other links, for About the Author; and your books, each
with its page in each store, for Also By (a series in order). A link put under the wrong store is
pointed out. While Scivelle is locked, the profile is sealed with your Library.

**Generate…** makes the editions you tick into one folder you choose (the last one is offered
again): *Title - Paperback 5.5x8.5.pdf*, *Title - Kindle.epub*, *Title - Apple Books.epub*,
*Title - Kobo.epub*, *Title - Google Play.epub*, *Title - Barnes and Noble.epub*, *Title - EPUB.epub*
and *Title - Submission.docx* (or .pdf). Each ebook is checked first — the package, every page
well-formed, the contents, the links, the identifiers, and the store's link rule — and anything
a store might turn away is listed before you choose to go on. A file already there is replaced
only when you say so.

A book the licence keeps read-only (after the trial, or a novel on a student licence) opens in
Publish with its forms read-only, and Generate still makes its editions.

**First-line indents.** A paragraph style can indent the first line of its paragraphs
(Format ▸ Styles ▸ Modify, *First line indent*). The standard manuscript and APA, MLA and Chicago
use half an inch. The book look uses a smaller one, with the first paragraph after a heading or a
scene break starting flush, as books are set. Word files keep the indent both ways. A manuscript
from Word that indents paragraph by paragraph is read with the indent in its Normal style.

## 8. Files, versions and recovery

**Documents in tabs.** A document you open comes up as a tab of the window you are in, beside
the **Home** tab (the Start Page and your Library), as in Safari: a book or document opened from
Home (the shelf, the Library, Recent or Open there) gets a tab of its own; one opened from a
document's tab (File ▸ Open, Recent, Import Book) takes that tab's place, its unsaved changes asked
about first; open one that is already open and its tab comes to the front instead. The tab strip across the top shows each document's name, a dot while it has unsaved changes (× to close on hover), a lock for a
locked document, a book while it is read in reading mode and a speaker while it is read aloud.
⌘T, or the strip's +, opens a new tab at the Start Page: whatever you open, bring in or begin there (a
wizard, a blank page, a book from the Library) takes that tab. ⌘N starts a blank document in a new tab;
Home's *Novel Writings* and *Academia Writings* choices (a novel, a story, a thesis, an essay) begin in
a new tab with their wizard. ⇧⌘N shows the Start Page in the tab you are in, the document kept behind it
(its unsaved changes asked about first), so the tab can take something else; ⌘1 brings Home to the front. ⌃Tab and
⌃⇧Tab move between tabs, ⌘1–⌘7 and ⌘9 go to a tab by its place (Home is ⌘1, ⌘9 the last), ⌘W
closes the tab in front (its unsaved sheet first), ⇧⌘W closes the window and asks about each
unsaved tab in turn, ⌥⌘W closes every tab but Home. Drag a tab to put it elsewhere in the strip, onto another window's
strip to move it there, or out of the window to give it a window of its own; Window ▸ Move Tab to
New Window and Merge All Windows do the same from the menu, and View ▸ Hide Tab Bar hides the
strip until the next tab. A double-click on the strip's empty space zooms the window, as a title
bar does. A saved document's tab untouched for an hour gives its page up and comes back, read
again, when you click it. If you would rather have a window for each document, Settings ▸ General
▸ *Open documents in* ▸ *A window each* brings that back: ⌘N then opens a window, and the Start
Page lives in each.

**The Scivelle file (.scivelle).** A new document is saved as a Scivelle file: everything about the
work in one file, so that opening it, on this Mac or another, brings all of it back:
- the text, with its styles, page, header and footer, and comments;
- the corkboard's and outliner's cards, the sticky notes, the plot grid, the notes, the format and
  the details;
- the cast;
- the document's own settings: what it is (a novel, a thesis…), its author, and your export choices;
- the Universe's world and runs;
- the version history: the branches and every version you kept.

To send the work to someone else, export it: **File ▸ Export As Word, Web Page or Text…**, or Export
to PDF, Export Submission Manuscript, Export Ebook (EPUB) or Export Print-Ready PDF (Paperback). The
Scivelle file stays as it is. A Word document you open keeps saving as Word; **File ▸ Save As…**
and *Scivelle Document* turns it into a Scivelle file, its versions with it. A Scivelle file can be
locked with a password like a Word file (File ▸ Lock…): the whole file is sealed, versions and all,
and it opens with its password, Touch ID or your recovery key.

- **AutoSave** is on unless you turn it off (the title bar's switch, or Settings ▸ General ▸
  Saving): a moment after you stop typing, the document is saved. A new document is saved into
  Scivelle's Library on its first change, named after its title. Saving, by AutoSave or ⌘S, keeps
  no version: the document is always the text as it is now (see Version History).
- **Where it is saved.** A new document is saved into Scivelle's own Library unless you choose
  otherwise: it then stays on your shelf and is guarded by Scivelle's lock. Choosing another
  folder asks first — *Save in Scivelle's Library*, *Save Here Anyway*, or Cancel — since a file
  there is protected only by a lock of its own (File ▸ Lock…); *Don't warn me again* stops asking.
- **Version History** (*History* in the Home tab, or File ▸ Version History…) holds the versions
  you keep, and opens by itself beside a saved document you open to edit. A version is the whole
  document: the text, and beside it the corkboard's and outliner's cards (synopses, labels,
  statuses, targets), the sticky notes, the plot grid, the notes and the cast. A change to any of
  them counts as a change. Merging a branch merges them too: what only the branch changed is taken,
  and where main and the branch both changed the same card, note or character, main's is kept and
  you are told. **Compare** says what differs beside the text as well ("Beside the text, they
  differ in 2 synopses, a sticky note, Mara in the cast"). Your writing is saved as you go, so a version is kept only when you want one:
  **Create Version** (beside *Compare versions…*) asks for a name — one of two words is offered,
  "Quiet harbour", ready to keep with Enter or type over ("First draft finished") — and keeps the
  text as it is now under it on the branch you are on (a document never saved goes into the Library first; nothing changed since the last
  version, nothing kept), and beginning, leaving or merging a branch keeps one too. About fifty are
  kept: the oldest go first, but never a branch's newest, the version a branch began at, or a
  merge. It opens beside the page: the versions newest first, each its name over its branch (in the
  branch's colour) and how long ago ("darker-ending · 2 hours ago"; the date, words and size in the
  tooltip) — the newest on the branch you are on is where you are, its dot a ring and "now" under
  its name (a branch with no version of its own yet shows a *Now* line); a merge is named 'Merged
  "darker-ending"', a revert 'Took "darker-ending" back out', a version from before names a
  name of two words of its own; *Rename version…* in its right-click menu renames it — drawn as a tree the way VS Code draws
  a project's history — main down the first lane, each branch on a lane of its own in its colour,
  splitting off the version it began at (a branch begun on another branch splits off that one),
  its name beside its newest version, a merge marked "merged" and the branch it took in, a revert
  "reverted merge of" it. Choose one
  and it shows on the page, read only, with a line in the pane saying which; *Back to now* there,
  Esc, or *Now* puts your text back as it was, unsaved changes and undo included; ↑ and ↓ step
  through them. Right-click a version for *Show on the page* (or *Back to now*), *Compare with
  now*, *Branch from this version*, *Restore this version* (it becomes the document), *Switch to*
  its branch when it is another's, and its branch's actions; the version you are on offers *New
  branch from here* instead.
- **Branches** try something apart without losing main: another ending, a darker chapter seven.
  The branch button (beside the branch's name, over the list) begins one where the document
  stands, as does *New branch from here* in the right-click menu of the version you are on;
  *Branch from this version* in an older version's right-click menu begins one there, and the
  document becomes it. A new branch is named at once
  with two words, as git hosts do (*quiet-margin*, *amber-sparrow*); rename it with the pencil
  beside it, in the branch menu, or from a copy's right-click menu. The button with the branch's
  name says which one the document is on; choose another there to switch — your work is saved on
  the branch you leave, and the document becomes the other's newest copy. Saves are kept on the
  branch you are on. Merge a branch into main with the merge button (beside the pencil while you
  are on it, or beside it in the branch menu) or *Merge … into main* in a copy's right-click menu:
  its changes come in paragraph by paragraph, from where the two last agreed, and a paragraph only
  one side changed takes that side's, and changes to different paragraphs come together even
  side by side (a paragraph reworded on one, a new one beside it on the other). Where both changed
  the same paragraph, both stand in the text — main's marked blue, the branch's green — and you
  right-click either one to *Keep Main's*, *Keep* the branch's, or *Keep Both* (deleting one side
  by hand keeps the other just the same); each choice is one edit (⌘Z undoes it). While places
  wait, the tree shows the merge at once, at the top of main ahead of the branch — *Merging “…”*,
  a dashed ring with the branch's lane running into it, and how many places are left to settle —
  and it is kept as main's newest version, drawn joining the two lanes, once every place is
  settled (or when you switch branches). Delete a branch from the same menus once
  you are on another (main itself cannot be deleted): a merged one's versions stay in main's
  history; an unmerged one's go with it.
- **Merges** (the tab beside *Versions*, once there is one) lists every merge, newest first: which
  branch went into which and when, how many paragraphs came from the branch by themselves, how
  many places both had changed and how each was settled. *See changes* shows it side by side —
  main before the merge beside main after it — and only that: its two sides are named, not chosen,
  with no menus and no swap, so a click cannot turn it into another comparison (*Compare
  versions…* compares any two). *Revert…* takes what the merge brought in back out
  and keeps everything you wrote since (a paragraph you changed since then stands both ways, now
  in blue and as before the merge in green, for you to choose by right-click); the result is kept
  as a new version marked "reverted merge of …", so it can be undone in turn, and the merge is
  shown as taken back out. The branch can then be merged again.
- **Compare versions** (in Version History, or *Compare with now* in a version's right-click
  menu) puts two branches side by side, in place of the page, each column named at its top: main
  on the left and the branch you are on on the right, say. Each name is the choice of what that
  column shows — click it for the branches (the one you are on is the text as it is now, any other
  its newest version) and *Earlier versions*, by branch and time; ⇄ between them swaps the two.
  Under them, a line says what changed over the whole (words added and removed, in how many
  chapters), with the arrows for the previous and next change (or P and N), *Changes only*, and
  *Done* (or Esc). The comparison takes the place of the page and the Manuscript pane (back as it
  was when you are done), as a split diff in VS Code does: the whole text of both, chapter by
  chapter, each paragraph across from its counterpart, compared two ways — neither side counts as
  the first. What only the left side has is blue, what only the right side has is green (as a
  merge marks main's and the branch's), each across from a hatched gap; a paragraph both have but
  word differently is tinted on both sides, each side's own words marked darker in its colour; the
  key under the summary names the two colours. Down the left, *Sections* lists every chapter —
  *Same*, *Different*, or *Only in* one side — with a dot (blue: only the left's, green: only the
  right's, half and half: both, worded differently; grey: the same) and each side's own words in its
  colour; the one in view is marked, and a click goes to it (a different one opens on its first
  difference). Down the right edge, a narrow ruler is the whole comparison: each difference marked
  on its side's half (blue left, green right, both for a paragraph worded differently) and a box for
  the part in view. It is the comparison's scrollbar: drag the box to scroll, click anywhere on it
  to go there (and drag on), or scroll over it.
  *Changes only* folds the unchanged paragraphs and chapters away. When the two share an earlier version — a branch
  and the main it began from — each side marks only what it changed since then: the branch's own
  edits on its side, main's later edits on main's, and a paragraph neither touched is plain on both
  (one a side took out shows plain across from "taken out on …"), so a branch never looks changed
  by what was written on main after it began. A document
  without Heading 1s is compared by its top headings.
  It only shows: a branch's changes come into main by merging it.
- **Recent** (Home tab) lists the documents opened or saved lately; *Clear Recent* empties it.
- **Markdown and plain text** keep the text and the basic formatting; use `.docx` to keep
  everything.
- **Cloud.** A document kept in iCloud Drive, Dropbox or OneDrive — and a series file beside it
  — syncs as any file does.

Scivelle ▸ Settings… (⌘,) has four tabs:

- **General**

  and *Make a New Recovery Key…* (see Locks).

## 11. Tips and troubleshooting

**About Scivelle** (Scivelle ▸ About Scivelle) shows the version you have and whether it came from
the website or the Mac App Store, your Mac (macOS, chip, languages), how the writing checks
stand right now — spelling, the grammar check, Improve Wording, Apple Intelligence, the voices,
your licence — and what each version of Scivelle brought. If something doesn't work, **Copy
details** puts all of it on the clipboard to paste into an email to support@scivelle.com; the
window itself sends nothing.

- **"Apple Intelligence is not available."** Turn it on in System Settings ▸ Apple Intelligence
  & Siri (macOS 26 or later, Apple silicon), or connect a service in Assistant Settings instead.
- **"Ollama isn't running."** Open the Ollama app, then *Check again* in Assistant Settings. If it has
  no models yet, run `ollama pull gemma3` in Terminal once.
- **A key is not accepted.** Keys are long and easy to cut short; copy it again from the
  provider's key page and paste it whole. A ChatGPT sign-in that has expired is renewed with
  *Continue with ChatGPT* again.
- **The page number in the status bar** follows the window as you scroll, and the caret when you
  click or type.
- **Words today** counts from the first time you open the document each calendar day; closing
  and reopening the app keeps the count.
- **Something went wrong in the text.** ⌘Z undoes it. File ▸ Version History… has every version
  you created, and one from each time you began, switched or merged a branch: choose one to see
  it, then *Restore this version*.
- **A Word file looks odd.** Scivelle ignores impossible values it meets in a file (an indent
  wider than the page, say); saving the file once writes it back clean.
- **"Trial · offline".** During the free trial Scivelle checks the date online when you open a
  document. When it gets no answer, the document stays readable, printable and exportable but
  not editable; connect to the internet and click *Try Again* in the banner. A licence needs no
  such check.

## 12. Privacy, in short

Scivelle keeps your documents wherever you save them and its settings on your Mac. It has no
account, no analytics and no tracking, and nothing about your writing. On its own it sends only these:
- **the licence key**, when you activate it, to Scivelle's licence service, with your Mac's name
  and a one-way code for it, so the licence works on this Mac; and about once a week to the store
  that sold it;
- **during the free trial**, a one-way code for your Mac when you open a document, to the
  licence service. It answers with the date and remembers the day your Mac's trial began, for a
  year, so installing again does not start a new trial;
- **a question to GitHub** once a day about a newer version, which you can turn off. Your
writing leaves your Mac only for a service you chose, and then only to that service, under its
terms: a provider you connected, when you run the assistant with it, and OpenAI's voices or a
speech server on another computer, when Listen reads in them (they get the passage being read).
A Scholar search sends only the words you searched, to OpenAlex;
an Internet Archive search, only the words you searched, to archive.org, and a recording you
play streams from there.
See the privacy policy for the details.
