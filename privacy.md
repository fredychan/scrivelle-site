# Privacy Policy

_Last updated: 5 November 2026_

Scivelle is a word processor for novelists, made by Lithovane LLC ("we"). This policy explains what the app
does with your writing and your data. The short version: your documents stay wherever you save
them, the app has no account, no analytics and no tracking (the one thing kept is the free
trial's one-way code for your computer and the day its trial began), and your writing leaves your Mac only when you use a service you chose for it,
such as a writing assistant you connect (ChatGPT or Claude, for example) or OpenAI's voices — and
then only to that service. What else the app sends, such as the update check and the licence
check, is listed below.

## What Scivelle stores, and where

- **Your documents** are files on your Mac (or in a cloud folder you keep them in). Scivelle
  reads and writes them when you open and save, and nowhere else.
- **Version history**: the versions of a document you create (and one each time you begin,
  switch or merge a branch), kept in the app's own data folder on your Mac so you can restore an
  earlier version. These copies never leave your Mac.
- **Settings and preferences** (appearance, accent colour, the daily word goal, what the
  assistant reads, the recent documents list, whether to look for updates) are kept on your Mac.
- **The trial and the licence**: the date of the first launch (also kept in a second small
  file in Library/Application Support, so the trial survives emptying the app's folder) and,
  once bought, the licence key and its certificate for this Mac. These stay on your Mac.
  Scivelle's licence service keeps, for a year, a one-way code for your Mac and the day its
  trial began (see *The free trial*).
- **Writing-service connections**: keys you paste and the sign-in tokens from "Continue with ChatGPT" or
  "Continue with OpenRouter" are kept in the app's data folder on your Mac, readable by your user
  account only, and sent only to the service they belong to. Disconnecting removes them.

Scivelle has no user accounts, no analytics, no advertising, no crash reporting and no
telemetry, and nothing about your writing reaches the developer.

## When text leaves your Mac

**Apple Intelligence.** By default the writing assistant uses Apple's on-device language model.
Your text is processed on your Mac and is not sent anywhere.

**Writing services you connect.** If you connect a provider in Settings — OpenAI (ChatGPT), Anthropic
(Claude), Google (Gemini), OpenRouter, or any server you name — then, each time you run an
assistant task, Ask, or a cast tool, Scivelle sends that provider the text the task needs:

- with *Around the caret*, the passage you selected or the paragraph at the caret and about
  1,400 words around it;
- with *The whole book*, your manuscript (up to 80,000 words), your cast notes and your world
  notes.

That text is handled under the provider's own terms and privacy policy, not this one. Scivelle
sends it only when you ask for an answer, only to the provider you chose, and never to the
developer. The assistant pane always says which service is answering.

**Scholar.** When you search in Insert ▸ Scholar, the words you typed go to OpenAlex
(api.openalex.org, run by the nonprofit OurResearch), the open index of scholarly papers, books
and theses, together with Scivelle's support address, which OpenAlex asks apps to give. Nothing
else is sent: not your document, not who you are. Nothing is sent until you search. The
sources you cite are kept in your document. *Open in Google Scholar* and *DOI* open
pages in your browser, under those sites' own terms.

**Reading a paper from Scholar.** When you click *Read* on a result with a free copy, Scivelle
downloads that copy straight from where it is kept — arXiv, PubMed Central, a university
repository or the publisher — so that host sees a request for that one file from your Mac, as it
would if you opened it in your browser. Only the paper is asked for: nothing about you or your
document goes with it. The paper is kept on your Mac in Scivelle's Library (sealed with the
rest when Scivelle is locked), and quoting from it into your document happens on your Mac. A PDF
you read in Scivelle is kept in the Library beside its book, to show its pages as printed: on your
Mac only, sealed when Scivelle is locked, and moved to the Trash when you delete the book.

**The Library's browser.** *Google Scholar* in the Library, and a publisher's page opened from
Papers, are web pages shown inside Scivelle, as your browser would show them: the sites see what
you search and click there, and may set their own cookies, under their own terms. Scivelle does
not read those pages or send anything about them anywhere, but for the address and title of each
page you visit, kept on your Mac as the browser's *History* (sealed with the rest while Scivelle
locks itself) for you to visit again. The History, cookies and sign-ins are kept on your Mac, apart
from the rest of Scivelle, until you take a page out or press *Clear All* in History (a sign-in a
site would end when the browser quits is kept a month, so you stay signed in). With *Private* on,
none of that is kept: it is forgotten when you turn Private off or quit Scivelle. A PDF or EPUB you
download there is kept in your Library like a paper from *Read*. *Cite This Paper…* sends only
the link or the title you right-clicked to OpenAlex, as a search in Scholar would.

**The grammar check** (Review ▸ Grammar) sends each paragraph you pause on to the same engine,
one paragraph at a time, and only paragraphs you have just edited. It is on by itself only when
the engine runs on your Mac (Apple Intelligence, Ollama), where nothing leaves your computer; with
a provider that receives your text you switch it on yourself, and the app says where the
paragraphs go. **Improve Wording** (Review ▸ Improve Wording, off until you turn it on) sends each
paragraph you have just edited to a larger model, which sends back better versions of its
sentences: with Apple Intelligence on macOS 27, Apple's Private Cloud Compute, which by Apple's
design does not store your text or make it available to Apple; otherwise a writing service you
connected (the one you use for the assistant, or the first one connected). The app says which
one receives your paragraphs when you turn it on. **The
meanings shown under corrected words** come from your Mac's own Dictionary and never leave your
computer. **The cast watcher** (the Watch button on the Cast tab, off until you turn it on)
likewise sends each paragraph you pause on that names a character whose traits you set, with
that character's profile, to the same engine, and **the world watcher** (the "Watch the world's
rules" checkbox on the Notes tab, off until you tick it) sends each paragraph you pause on, with
one rule of your world at a time, to the same engine.

**Ollama** runs on your Mac; text sent to it does not leave your computer.

**Other writing checkers.** To keep Grammarly's suggestions and Scivelle's from covering each
other, Scivelle asks macOS whether Grammarly is open. It asks about Grammarly only, by its app
identifier, learns nothing else about what runs on your Mac, and sends nothing anywhere.

**The licence key.** When you activate a licence, Scivelle sends the key, a name for your Mac
(its computer name) and a code for your Mac to Scivelle's licence service at scivelle.com. The
service passes the key and the name to the store that sold it, to check the key and count the
activation. It sends back a certificate for this Mac, which Scivelle keeps and checks each time
it starts. The code is made on your Mac from its hardware ID and the key, one way, so the ID
cannot be worked out from it, and it is different for every key. The service keeps nothing you
send it, and like any website it sees your Mac's internet address. About once a week, when
online, Scivelle checks the key with the store again. Nothing else is sent, and nothing about
your documents. Deactivating tells the store to release this Mac.

**The free trial.** When the trial starts, and when you open a document during it (at most
twice a day), Scivelle asks its licence service for the date, so the trial cannot be stretched
by changing the Mac's clock. With the question go a code for your Mac and the day Scivelle has
as the trial's first. The code is made on your Mac from its hardware ID, one way, so the ID
cannot be worked out from it. It is Scivelle's own and matches nothing else, not even your
licence's code. The service remembers the code and the day your Mac's trial began, for a year,
so deleting Scivelle and installing it again does not start a new trial. They are kept in the
service's database (Upstash, connected through Vercel). That is all it keeps: nothing about
you, your documents or what you do in Scivelle, and like any website it sees your Mac's
internet address. Once the service knows your Mac, Scivelle asks the store for the date instead
when the service cannot be reached; that request carries nothing at all. When neither answers,
documents open read-only until one does. The Mac App Store version has no trial or keys and
makes none of these requests.

**Checking for a newer version.** Once a day, the direct-download version asks the place the
downloads live (GitHub Releases) whether a newer Scivelle exists. The request carries nothing
about you or your documents; GitHub sees your Mac's internet address, as any website you visit
does. An update is downloaded only when you choose to. You can turn the daily check off in
Settings ▸ Updates. The Mac App Store version is updated by the App Store and never checks.

**Sign in with ChatGPT.** "Continue with ChatGPT" opens OpenAI's sign-in page in your browser.
Scivelle receives tokens that let it use your ChatGPT plan, and your account's email address to
show you who is signed in. It never sees your password or your ChatGPT conversations. Signing
out revokes the tokens.

**Spelling.** The text on the page is checked by your Mac's own spelling checker, on your Mac,
with the dictionaries and the learned words macOS keeps; *Learn Spelling* adds a word to that
list, which every app on your Mac shares. The other text fields use the browser engine's
checker, whose dictionaries are downloaded once, for your language, from the dictionary server
used by the Chromium browser engine inside the app. No text of yours is sent for spell checking.

**The free library.** Its list of public-domain books is fetched from a public library's
catalogue feed when you first open it, and again when the copy on your Mac is a week old;
browsing and searching it happen on your Mac. A book is downloaded only when you choose
*Download free*, from a public library mirror. Neither request carries anything about you or
your documents; the servers see your Mac's internet address, as any website you visit does.

**The Internet Archive.** The Library's Internet Archive section finds scanned books that are out
of copyright at the Internet Archive (archive.org, a nonprofit library). Nothing is sent until you
search, choose one of its suggested searches or choose one of its categories; then your search
words, or the category's subjects, go to archive.org's search. Choosing a book reads its record there, and its scan is downloaded from there only when
you choose *Download*. Its Audiobooks, Films and Radio work the same way: your search words, or
the category chosen, go to archive.org's search; choosing a recording reads its record there, and
the pictures of the recordings found come from there; what you play streams from there as you
listen or watch, and nothing of it is downloaded or kept. Where you stopped in each recording is
kept on your Mac only, to go on from there, with a small copy of its picture, asked of archive.org
once when you first play it, so the list of those you left partway shows it without asking again;
none of it is ever sent. None of these requests carries
anything about you or your documents; the Internet Archive sees your Mac's internet address, as
any website you visit does.

**Opening links.** Links you click in the app (a provider's key page, say) open in your
browser.


## This website

Apart from the free trial's code, the app keeps nothing about you; the website, scivelle.com, counts
its visitors. It uses Vercel Web
Analytics, from the company that hosts the site, to count visits and page views and to see
which pages are read, the country a visit comes from, and the kind of browser and device. It
sets no cookies and keeps nothing that identifies you: a visit is recognised for one day by a
value computed from the request, then forgotten, and nothing is shared for advertising or
followed across other sites. Nothing you write in Scivelle ever reaches the website.

## Children

Scivelle is not directed to children and collects no personal information from anyone.
